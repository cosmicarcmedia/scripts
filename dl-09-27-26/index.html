1
The training I could have taken anywhere else
The origin and the face
▾
Builds Dr. Lewis as a specific, credentialed person before anything sells. Right now the practice's own marketing is an AI voice and a front desk perspective. Nobody sees the actual surgeon.
Spot A
I trained at two of the most competitive surgical programs in the country. UT Southwestern for medical school and general surgery. Then Vanderbilt for plastic surgery. I could have taken that training anywhere. Dallas. Nashville. Wherever paid the most. Except I came back to San Angelo and never left.
Spot B
That was the early nineties. Same office downtown, a couple blocks off the Concho River, ever since. I still do every consultation myself. Not a coordinator. Not a nurse first and the doctor later. Me, from the first conversation to the last follow up appointment. You tell me what's bothering you, and I'm the one who has to look you in the eye and tell you if it's a good idea. This isn't a franchise with rotating doctors. It's one guy who has to live in this town and answer for his own work.
Spot C
I still hold privileges at every hospital and surgery center in this town. That's not automatic. Hospitals check a surgeon's outcomes before they hand that over. So if you need real reconstructive work, you don't have to drive to Dallas or Austin to get it done right. Thirty plus years in, I get patients who ask if I remember doing their mother's surgery back in the nineties. More often than you'd think, I do.
2
Why people drive in from Midland and Abilene
The local anchor
▾
Turns his own service area into a real reason to pick him over a four hour round trip or a nearby spa, using the actual towns people drive from.
Spot B
People drive in from Midland and Abilene for this. Not because San Angelo is on the way to anywhere. Because there isn't another option in between.
Spot A
I set up my practice here on purpose, back in the early nineties. A couple blocks off the Concho River, downtown. Not in Dallas. Not in Austin. Here. If you live out in this part of the state and you need real surgical care, cosmetic or reconstructive, you shouldn't have to drive four hours just to get a consultation. West Texas is a big stretch of highway between anywhere and anywhere else. That drive matters more than people think when you're already anxious about a surgery. I got tired of hearing distance as the reason people put off care they needed.
Spot C
So this office does both ends of it. The surgical side. Tummy tucks, breast surgery, reconstructive work after an injury or a cancer surgery. And the non surgical side too. Botox, fillers, CoolSculpting, the skin treatments. You don't need a surgeon two hours away for one appointment and a spa across town for the other. It's one office, same doctor, same building for over thirty years. San Angelo isn't a stopover between two bigger cities to me. It's the reason I set up shop here, and the reason I stayed.
3
What a med spa can't do when something goes wrong
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
The sharpest of the four. Names a real structural gap, no surgeon on staff and no accountability if an injectable goes wrong, that an injectable only storefront cannot answer on camera, without naming anyone.
Spot C
A lot of places doing Botox and fillers around here don't have a surgeon on staff at all. They have a medical director who signs off from somewhere else. And a room of injectors who've never done surgery in their life.
Spot A
That's legal. I'm not saying it isn't. But if something goes wrong, an injector can't fix it. They send you somewhere else, and now you're starting over with a stranger who doesn't know what was used or why. I don't have that problem. I'm the surgeon, and I'm also the one doing your Botox. If I see something in a screening that a spa would never catch, I'm trained to catch it, because surgery is the job here, and Botox is just part of the visit.
Spot B
Same goes for the bigger procedures. Reconstructive work, tummy tucks, breast surgery. Some practices have you meet the surgeon for the first time the morning of your procedure. I've done your consultation, your pre-op, and your follow up for over thirty years running, same guy every time. I'm also credentialed at every hospital and surgical center in this town, which takes real vetting to get. Hospitals check a surgeon's outcomes before they hand over privileges like that. If something doesn't look right three weeks after surgery, you call the same number you called to book it.
4
Three questions to ask before anyone touches your face
The useful one
▾
Costs the practice nothing to give away and works whether or not the viewer ever books here. A surgeon teaching people how to vet a provider is itself a credential.
Spot A
Before you let anyone put a needle in your face, ask three questions first. This applies whether you ever set foot in my office or not. It takes thirty seconds, and it matters more than people think.
Spot B
Ask who is giving you the injection, by name, not just whose name is on the sign outside. Ask what happens if you have a reaction. Who you call, and whether they can see you that same day. Ask how long that specific person has been doing that specific treatment. Not medicine in general. That treatment. One more. If the price seems too low to be real, ask why. Injectables have a real cost per syringe, and a number that low usually means it's diluted, or it isn't the product they told you it was.
Spot C
Here's why I'm telling you this, even though I'd rather you just book with me. Most of the bad outcomes people bring to me to fix didn't happen because of a bad product. They happened because nobody asked those questions before they sat down. So ask them, wherever you end up going. I'm not trying to scare you out of getting something done. I'm trying to make sure whoever you pick has to answer for it if it goes wrong. And if you'd rather ask me those same three questions in person, my door is downtown, and I'll answer every one myself.
