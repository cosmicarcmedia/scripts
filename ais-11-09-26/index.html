Before November of 2014, I worked in the oilfield

2
21 inches a year, and some of it comes in a day
The local anchor
▾
Rain is the one thing every customer in the Concho Valley shares, and about 21 inches a year with five or six sometimes landing in a day is a specific local fact that puts tanks, drip and pivots into one story.
Spot B
San Angelo averages about 21 inches of rain a year. A single downpour can drop five or six inches in a day. Then it can go a long stretch with nothing. So a lot of what we sell here does one of two jobs. It catches the downpour, or it makes the dry stretch last.
Spot A
Start with catching it. The Texas Water Development Board figures a 2,000 square foot roof sheds roughly 1,000 gallons for every inch of rain. That counts on about 80 percent of it reaching the tank. So five inches in a day is about 5,000 gallons off that roof. Without a tank, most of that runs off the property. A tank holds it for the weeks the rain doesn't show up. We carry poly tanks from 100 gallons up to 5,000.
Spot C
For ranches, there are Pioneer tanks that hold tens of thousands of gallons. What does one look like? A giant grain bin. There's one near Rowena that holds 65,567. [OWNER: your part in the Rowena tank, and whether the farm is fine being named] Making it last is the other half. That's drip irrigation, and center pivots for the farmers around here. For ranchers, there's Ranchbot, which shows a tank's water level on your phone. That's fewer trips out to check a tank. Everybody out here is working with the same 21 inches. [OWNER: a big rain you remember out here] Tell me what you're trying to water, and we'll figure out the rest.
3
Ask whoever quotes you a rain tank for one number
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
A quote that covers only the tank hides the piping and the filter, so four plain questions any customer can ask any seller draw a fair line without naming anyone, and that is the script a neighbor forwards to a neighbor.
Spot C
Before you buy a rain tank, ask the seller what the whole system costs per gallon. I mean the tank, the piping from your gutters, and the filter, all in one number. A quote on just the tank leaves out the rest of the system. [OWNER: confirm you still quote a rain system as one price per gallon, or change the next line to match how you quote today] That's how we quote it: tank, piping and filtration together, priced by the gallon. Right now that comes to [OWNER: today's price per gallon for tank, piping and filtration. Your 2023 CALF News range was $1 to $1.50.].
Spot A
Next, ask where the tank is going. Piping to a tank in the back costs more than a tank beside the downspout. That's real money, and it belongs on the quote. Then ask what's in the filter. A typical setup runs a sediment filter, then carbon, then a UV light. A typical system handles 15 to 20 gallons a minute. If the water's headed into your house, that filter belongs on the quote too.
Spot B
Last, ask how many gallons your roof will catch in a year. If they can't do that math, they're guessing at the tank size. We start with three things: your roof, the rain, and what you plan to use the water for. Then we pick a tank to fit. It's not real complex or difficult. It just takes somebody to walk you through it. Ask the next guy those four questions, then come ask us the same four.
4
How many gallons your roof catches, with a tape measure
The useful one
▾
Every rain tank decision starts with one number a homeowner can get with a tape measure, and because the steps and the 0.623 factor already sit on the store's own rainwater calculator, the video costs nothing to give away and sends the viewer to the site and then to the counter.
Spot A
You can work out how much water your roof catches with a tape measure and a calculator. Measure the length of the roof, then the width. Measure it flat, like you're looking down from above. Don't worry about the slope. Length times width gives you square feet. If you've got more than one roof section, measure each one and add them up. A barn counts too.
Spot B
Say your roof is 40 feet by 30 feet. That's 1,200 square feet. Now multiply by the inches of rain, and then by point six two three. That number is just how many gallons one inch of rain leaves on a square foot. For one inch of rain, that roof gets about 750 gallons. No roof catches every drop. The Texas Water Development Board figures about 80 percent makes it to the tank. So call it 600 gallons for every inch.
Spot C
San Angelo averages about 21 inches a year. In an average year, that roof catches around 12,500 gallons. That's the number you size a tank around. For scale, an inch of rain off 100 square feet of roof fills a 50 gallon barrel. That's a small shed. Your house is a lot more. The calculator on our website does this math for you. Put in your roof and your rainfall, and it hands you gallons. Then you know what to ask before you shop for a tank.
