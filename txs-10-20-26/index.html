1 Texas lets you skip workers comp Visitor Center front entrance 68s

I've written commercial insurance in Texas for over twenty years, and workers comp is the coverage people talk themselves out of fastest. Because in Texas, for most private businesses, it isn't required. That surprises people who come here from out of state. Texas is a non-subscriber state, so a lot of employers can legally opt out.

And when an owner hears that, the next thought is usually, great, one less bill. So let me ask you something. Say you're opening this year, you expect to do nine hundred thousand in sales, and you've got seventeen people on payroll. One of them gets hurt on the job.

Who pays for that? Not required just means nobody makes you buy it. The injury still happens either way. That medical bill and that lost time still land somewhere, and without a policy, they land on the business.

That's the part I want owners to see before they decide. Required and needed are two different questions. Ask yourself both before you skip it.

2 Your 1099 guys might be on your policy anyway Visitor Center front entrance 70s

At Texas Select we talk to contractors every week, and there's one line I hear more than any other. I don't have employees, I just have 1099 guys. And I get it. For the IRS, that might be exactly right.

But your insurance carrier doesn't always look at it the way the IRS does. Then the year ends, and the carrier audits your general liability policy. They look at every sub you paid. If that sub brought their own certificate of insurance, great.

If they didn't, the auditor can treat them like they were your employee. Which means you pay premium to cover a guy you thought you'd hired out. Does that seem fair? Maybe not, but look at it from the carrier's side.

If he gets hurt or breaks something on your job and he's got no insurance, whose policy do you think takes the claim? Yours. So collect the certificate before he starts, and make sure it lists you as additional insured. Keep it on file, and your audit goes a whole lot smoother.

3 The insurance bill that shows up after the policy ends Visitor Center front entrance 65s

We watched a business owner get a bill for twenty thousand dollars after his policy had already expired. He'd paid every premium on time. So where did twenty grand come from? The audit.

Most people treat their general liability premium like a truck payment, one number that never moves. That number moves. When we write that policy, a lot of it gets priced off estimates, your sales and your payroll for the year ahead. At the end of the term, the carrier looks at your real numbers.

If you did a lot more business than you told them, they bill you the difference. And that bill can be big. Growing is a good problem to have. Getting surprised by it isn't.

So if you're having a bigger year than you planned, call your agent halfway through. We can adjust the estimate midterm, and you pay as you go. That's a five minute phone call that can save you a five figure bill.

4 Guess high or guess low on your sales Visitor Center front entrance 67s

Every new business owner who sits down with us asks some version of the same question. Should I guess my sales high to be safe, or guess low and hang on to the cash? Guessing low feels smart. Lower estimate, lower premium today, more money in the bank.

But the audit at the end of the year runs on your real numbers, so that money comes due anyway, all at once. So guess high, right? Well, one of our account managers has been at this for years and has seen exactly one refund check. Carriers aren't in a hurry to send money back.

So the answer is neither. Give us your best honest guess. Then, about six months in, look at where you are. If you've already passed that number, call us.

We'll adjust it, or at least you'll know to start setting money aside. Either way, the audit doesn't catch you off guard. Your best guess plus one check in beats a lucky guess every time.

5 Why your commercial policy wants a new down payment Visitor Center front entrance 62s

Our commercial renewals team gets the same surprised phone call over and over. Why am I making a down payment again? It comes down to what kind of policy you have. A lot of commercial policies, especially the ones written through the surplus market, aren't continuous.

They don't just roll over to the next year. Every year it's a brand new policy. New paperwork, a fresh look at your business, and a new premium. And if you finance that premium, that means a new down payment every single year.

So how do you plan for it? Put the renewal date on the calendar like it's a bill, because it is one. Give your agent a heads up on what's changed, new trucks, new people, new kinds of work. That way the new premium matches the business you have now.

Nobody likes a surprise down payment. A calendar reminder takes care of most of them.

6 The most important page in your policy Visitor Center river patio 60s

I've spent over twenty years reading commercial policies, and I can tell you which page most people never look at. It isn't this one. This is the declarations page, the dec page. Your premium, your limits, your deductible.

It's the page everybody reads, because it tells you what you're paying. And what you pay matters, sure. Now flip the page. This right here is the exclusions.

So why is this the important one? Because this page tells you what the carrier won't pay for. It's not what you pay today. It's what they pay whenever you have an accident or a claim.

Two policies can have the exact same price on the front and completely different exclusions on the back. One covers the work you do. The other quietly carves it out. So before you compare prices, compare this page.

Ask your agent to walk you through every line of it.

7 The plumber who installed a check valve backwards Visitor Center river patio 69s

One of the most common exclusions I see on contractor policies is two words long. Your work. Picture a licensed plumber with a general liability policy. He installs a check valve backwards, so it lets water through, and something floods.

He's got insurance, so he's fine, right? Not if his policy carries a your work exclusion. That exclusion takes out the very thing he's licensed to do. And think about what that means for a plumber.

His work is plumbing. So the mistake most likely to happen on his jobs is the one the policy steps away from. He's paying for coverage on everything except what he does all day. We see this constantly with some of the carriers writing cheaper general liability.

They'll write you, and then exclude nearly everything you do. So how do you close that gap? For a lot of contractors, the answer is contractors errors and omissions coverage. Ask your agent if your policy has a your work exclusion, and if it does, ask what fills it.

8 When your advice becomes the claim Visitor Center river patio 77s

If you're a contractor who also gives advice, there's a page in your general liability policy worth finding today. It's usually around page four. A lot of contractors in Texas do more than swing hammers now. You're doing design build, you're managing projects, you're telling clients which material to use.

And the second you move from doing the work to planning it, the risk changes. Say you tell a client to swap a material to save money. Eighteen months later it fails, and the building starts to rot. Is that a general liability claim?

Your carrier may say no. Look for an endorsement called exclusion, contractors professional liability. It's often numbered CG 2279. It excludes damage that comes out of professional services, like drawings, inspections, or the advice you gave.

So the carrier could call that swap a professional error, and you'd be funding your own legal defense. That's what professional liability, or errors and omissions, is built for. It's triggered by financial loss from your advice. If you charge for your expertise, ask your agent whether your coverage follows you there.

Plenty of general contractors now require it from their subs anyway.

9 The roofer and the third story Visitor Center river patio 55s

If you're a general contractor hiring a roofer, there's one exclusion I'd check before he climbs anything. A height limitation. Some cheaper policies say the sub isn't covered above two stories. That's it.

Two stories. So picture your roofer on a three story house. He falls. Whose insurance pays?

His carrier pays zero if that exclusion's on his policy. And the lawsuit comes looking for you, the contractor who hired him. And here's the thing. That sub probably handed you a certificate of insurance.

You filed it. It looked fine. A lot of subs buy the cheapest policy they can find just to get that certificate and get on the job. So read it, or send it to your agent and let us read it.

Ask about height limits and residential exclusions before the ladder goes up.

10 Buying business insurance online at midnight Visitor Center river patio 68s

I spent about eighteen years as a captive agent before I went independent, so I've seen insurance sold just about every way it gets sold. And online is the easiest by far. You can get a quote at midnight in your pajamas. I'll give it that.

So what's missing? Nobody's asking you the right questions. Nobody's explaining the exclusions, and nobody's in your corner when a claim goes sideways. For a simple risk, that might be fine.

But a real business usually has a wrinkle somewhere, a kind of job, a contract requirement, a sub you use. And the online form doesn't know to ask about it. That's what an independent agent is for. We work with a lot of carriers, so we can match your business to the one whose policy fits it.

And we live in the claim world too, so we know which carriers are fair and which ones fight every little thing. Buying online is quick. Make sure somebody's read page two before you call it done.

11 Switching carriers to save a few bucks Celebration Bridge 64s

Every renewal season I watch contractors switch carriers to save a little on premium. Sometimes that's the right call, but there's one clause I want you to check before you sign. It's called a prior work exclusion. It says the new carrier only covers work that started after the new policy began.

Everything before that date belongs to somebody else. So walk it forward. You switch this year, and two years from now you get sued over a job you finished last spring. Who covers it?

The new carrier says they didn't insure that work. The old carrier says your policy with them expired. And you're standing in the middle with no coverage for any of the work you did before the switch. Is the savings worth that?

When you're comparing quotes, ask your agent one question. Does this policy have a prior acts or retroactive date limitation? If it does, find out exactly which date it starts from.

12 The railing that failed three years later Celebration Bridge 78s

We reviewed a case where a custom railing failed three years after the house was built. The homeowner fell, got hurt, and pulled the contractor back into a lawsuit. And the contractor said what you'd probably say. I finished that job.

I got paid, I walked away, so why is this hitting me now? Because your risk doesn't end when you leave the job site. In Texas you can be sued over construction defects for up to ten years after the work is substantially complete. Your general liability has two timelines in it.

Ongoing operations while you're on the job, and completed operations, which starts the day you hand over the keys. Some cheaper policies carry an endorsement numbered CG 2104. It excludes the products and completed operations hazard. In plain English, you're covered while you work and on your own once you leave.

So your coverage stops the day you leave, and your liability keeps going for ten years. Does your policy close that gap? Ask your agent if you have products and completed operations coverage. Don't let a small savings on premium turn into a claim you're paying yourself three years from now.

13 The box on a certificate nobody reads Celebration Bridge 64s

If you hire subs, you've got a stack of certificates of insurance somewhere. Have you read one? Most get filed the second they come in. But there's a box on every certificate that tells you whether that sub's policy protects you.

It's called the description of operations. Look for three things in there. Are you listed as additional insured? Is there a waiver of subrogation?

And is their coverage primary and noncontributory? Additional insured means their policy covers you for their work. The waiver keeps their carrier from paying a claim and then turning around and suing you. Primary and noncontributory means their insurance pays first and can't drag yours in to split it.

And check that the additional insured status includes completed operations as well as ongoing. The forms to look for are CG 2010 and CG 2037. If you don't see both, you're exposed. That's a two minute read.

It's a lot cheaper than finding out after a claim.

14 The sub whose policy lapsed on a Friday Celebration Bridge 55s

There's one date on every subcontractor's certificate I'd want every general contractor watching. The expiration date. Most people check it once, the day the sub starts. It's current, it looks good, it goes in the file.

But what if that policy expires halfway through your project? Say the sub lets it lapse on a Friday. On Monday their crew starts a fire on your job. Their coverage is gone, so the claim heads to you.

That's your policy, your limits, and your loss history taking the hit for their mistake. So build a simple system. Put every sub's expiration date on a calendar. A couple of weeks before it hits, ask for a renewal certificate.

No renewal certificate, no work on site until it shows up. It's boring paperwork, and it keeps somebody else's lapse off your record.

15 Hand your agent the contract first Celebration Bridge 62s

You just landed a big contract, and before you start, there's one email I'd love for you to send. Send your agent the contract. Most companies that hire contractors put their insurance requirements right in the master service agreement. Limits, endorsements, who has to be additional insured.

It's all spelled out in there. So what happens when nobody reads that section until day one? Your certificate gets kicked back. The job sits while everybody scrambles to fix the paperwork.

And sometimes the coverage they're asking for isn't something you can add overnight. Can you afford to lose that week? When we get the agreement ahead of time, we line your policy up against it. Anything that's missing gets handled before you ever show up on site.

That's how you start the job on day one and skip the scramble. So when the contract lands, forward it to us. It's the easiest thing you'll do all week.
