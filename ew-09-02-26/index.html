The scripts

1 Conrad Hilton built this and you can live in it Cactus Hotel 93s

Frasier spent eleven seasons in an apartment with a view he would not shut up about. You can do a version of that here, for San Angelo money, in the tallest building in town. This is the Cactus. It opened in 1929 as Conrad Hilton's fourth hotel.

Not his fourth in Texas. His fourth ever, back when the name meant a guy with a handful of buildings instead of a chain on six continents. Fourteen stories, a hundred sixty-five feet, two hundred fifty rooms, drawn by a Dallas architect named Anton Korn. Almost a century later nothing in this town has passed it. Hilton lost it in the Depression and sold it in 1932, it picked up the Cactus name in 1934, and it went on the National Register in 1984.

So what is in there now? Most people see a historic building downtown and assume it is a museum, or worse, that it is sitting empty. It is neither. There are offices in it.

The symphony is in it. There are apartments in it, and some of the units on the fourth and fifth floors still have the original 1929 tile on the bathroom walls. Think about that for a second. You can rent a piece of this town's actual history and then take an elevator down to work.

People drive past this building every single day and never once look up.

2 One man designed forty buildings down here Cactus Hotel 88s

An architect named Oscar Ruffini designed roughly forty buildings in downtown San Angelo. Not forty in his whole career. Forty here. And a bunch of them are still standing and still being used, which for a nineteenth century architect in a West Texas town is a genuinely absurd hit rate. Then you have Anton Korn.

He drew the two buildings you would point at first if somebody asked you to describe this skyline. The San Angelo National Bank building in 1927. The Cactus Hotel in 1929. Same architect, two years apart, both still here.

So what does that get you, other than a decent piece of trivia? It tells you this downtown is more of a set than it looks like. These buildings talk to each other because a handful of people drew all of them, in the same decade, in the same styles, out of the same materials. Once somebody points that out to you it is impossible to unsee walking down Chadbourne.

And if you are looking at buying down here, that is worth real money. It means the building you are considering has siblings. Somebody two doors down has already run into the problem you are about to run into, in a structure built the exact same way, and you can go ask them how it went.

3 You can own the property and not what is under it Cactus Hotel 102s

You can own a piece of property in Texas and not own what is underneath it. The house, the fence, the driveway, every square inch of the lot, all yours, and the ground under it belongs to somebody else entirely. Everybody has watched a show about West Texas oil by now, and this is the part those shows skip right past. The mineral estate can be split off from the surface and sold separately, and once that happens the two travel apart forever.

So what does that mean for whoever owns the surface? This is the part that gets people. The law treats the mineral estate as the dominant one. Not equal to the surface. Dominant.

So whoever owns the minerals under your yard has an implied legal right to come onto your surface and go get them, and they do not need to ask you first. Now, there are real limits. They have to give you notice before drilling a new well, they cannot be negligent about it, and there is a doctrine that protects you when they have a reasonable alternative and you do not. But the starting point is nothing like what most people picture when they hear the word own.

So when you are under contract, two things. Read the deed for reservation language, something like reserving all oil, gas and other minerals. And read Schedule B of your title commitment, because if the minerals came off this property any time in the last hundred years, that is where it shows up.

4 This dirt paid for two universities Cactus Hotel 91s

There is a patch of ground about an hour and a half west of here that nobody wanted. The state handed it off to the University of Texas back when West Texas land was considered close to worthless, and at the time that was a completely reasonable opinion. Then in May of 1923 a well called Santa Rita Number One came in on it, outside a town called Big Lake. First commercial oil well in the Permian Basin.

The first royalty check the university got was five hundred and sixteen dollars. So what happened next? In the twelve months after that well hit, the fund behind it grew four hundred and forty-five percent. It had never once cracked a million dollars in the forty years before that.

Today it is worth better than forty billion, it is the largest public university endowment in the country, and it still splits between the UT system and the A&M system. There are two point one million acres of West Texas ground across nineteen counties still feeding it. All of it off land people had written off as good for nothing but running goats. Which is the whole thing about dirt out here.

What a piece of ground is worth on top and what it is worth underneath are two completely separate questions. Almost nobody thinks to ask the second one.

5 The town name is a corrected typo Chadbourne block 92s

San Angelo is named after a spelling correction. I am not exaggerating for the video. The people who founded this place wanted to call it Santa Angela. The post office looked at that in 1883 and said no.

Not because another town already had it, and not for anything political. They rejected it on grammar. Santa Angela was the wrong construction in Spanish, so somebody fixed the ending, and here we are a hundred and forty years later living inside the correction. So why would that matter to anybody buying a house here?

Because it tells you exactly what kind of town this is. Nobody down here was working off a master plan. People showed up, put buildings where buildings made sense, and dealt with whatever came back in the mail. You can see it in the street grid.

You can definitely see it in the fact that a downtown this size has a fourteen story hotel sitting in the middle of it. There is a version of San Angelo that got designed by a committee, and I promise you it does not look like this one. That is the part I like about it. Every time somebody tells me a West Texas town has no story in it, I think about the fact that this one is named after a mistake somebody had to go back and fix.

6 The city told a bank no Chadbourne block 90s

In January of last year a bank asked the city if it could tear down a building downtown. The commission said no, six to nothing. Then the council backed them up, unanimously. The building is at 231 West Beauregard, and everybody around here believed it was coming down to make parking.

The mayor's line was that the city cannot keep pulling out buildings and leaving empty lots behind. I bring that up because people ask me all the time whether the historic rules down here have any actual teeth, or whether it is a suggestion you can pay your way past. It is not a suggestion. There is a district down here with real boundaries, running roughly from the river up to Fourth and between Irving and Oakes.

Own a designated building inside it and your exterior changes, your new construction and your demolition all go through a certificate of appropriateness. That is a review. A bank with lawyers just found that out in public. Now, is that a reason to stay away from downtown? No, and plenty of people are buying down here and doing fine.

It is a reason to find out exactly what is designated and what is not before you write the offer, because that one answer decides what you are allowed to do with the thing you just bought.

7 A whole neighborhood here does not own its own dirt Chadbourne block 96s

There is a neighborhood in this town where the people in those houses do not own the ground underneath them. Out at Lake Nasworthy, the lake and the land around it belong to the City of San Angelo. The city runs a lot lease program through its real estate division. So you own the house. You lease the dirt it sits on. And there is an annual fee on top of whatever you paid for the structure.

Now you might assume that is a technicality nobody enforces. It is not. In December of 2024 the city council took up terminating one of those leases and evicting the leaseholder over about three thousand dollars in unpaid fees. That was on the agenda, in a public meeting, in the minutes.

Am I telling you to stay away from the lake? Not even a little. Plenty of people out there love where they live and would not trade it for anything in town. I am telling you this because if you are looking at property on that water and nobody has explained the difference between owning and leasing to you yet, you are not being taken care of.

Three questions, and ask them out loud. What actually conveys at closing. What the annual lease fee is and when it last went up. And what happens at renewal, because that is the one that decides what this is worth to you in twenty years.

8 Ron Swanson would move here for the water law Chadbourne block 94s

Ron Swanson spent seven seasons arguing the government should have as little to do with your property as humanly possible. That man would love Texas groundwater law, right up until he did not. Because in Texas, the water underneath your land belongs to you. Not the state. You.

You can pump it, sell it, lease it, or haul it off the property in a truck. It is called the rule of capture, it goes back to a court decision in 1904, and around here people call it the law of the biggest pump. So where is the catch? Your neighbor's water is his the exact same way, and he can pump as much of it as he wants.

If he drills deeper than you and pumps harder than you and your well runs dry because of it, in Texas that is generally his water now. He does not owe you anything, unless he did it out of spite, or he is wasting it, or he was flat negligent. That is the default. What keeps this from being total chaos is the local groundwater district, which handles well spacing and permitting, and the one covering this county is headquartered out in Wall.

So if you are looking at a place with a well on it, ask how deep yours goes and what the wells around it are doing. The water is yours. So is the risk.

9 Hank Hill would understand this question Chadbourne block 91s

Hank Hill sold propane and propane accessories in a fictional Texas town called Arlen, and not one person watching that show ever thought that was a weird way to make a living. Out here it makes even more sense than it did on TV. The second you cross the city limit there is no gas line running to the house. The stove, the water heater, the furnace, the fireplace if you have one, all of it runs off a tank sitting in the yard, and somebody drives out a few times a year and fills it.

So here is the question almost nobody asks, and almost nobody asks it for them either. Do you own that tank, or is it leased from the propane company? Because if it is leased, you buy your propane from that company, at that company's price, and that is the arrangement until you do something about it. Own the tank outright and you can call around every fall and shop it.

Over one cold winter that is a real number. Over ten years it is a genuinely irritating number to find out about late. And it never shows up in a listing photo, it never comes up at a showing, and it is not going to be on the seller's disclosure in a way you would catch. Ask before you close.

Hank would.

10 This building was boarded up eighteen months ago Roosevelt Lofts 87s

Eighteen months ago this building was plywood. It opened as the Rainbow Hotel in 1929, got renamed the Roosevelt in 1933, and then it sat for a long, long time. Now it is twenty-five loft apartments and people live in them. Six and a half million dollars of renovation, doors opened last September, income restricted for seniors, and it filled.

So does anybody actually live downtown? That is the question I get more than almost any other one about this part of town, and the honest answer used to be not really. It is not that anymore. Put this building together with the apartments inside the Cactus and the number is going up, inside buildings that people had already written off as dead weight.

Now you might be thinking, fine, but I am never going to live downtown, so why do I care. Here is why. Occupied buildings hold value differently than empty ones. They keep a block alive after five o'clock, they are what makes a restaurant survive down here, and they change what the building next door is worth.

So if you have been carrying around a picture of downtown San Angelo as somewhere you visit and then drive home from, that picture is about two years stale, and it is going to keep getting worse.

11 Forty-five cents back on every rehab dollar Roosevelt Lofts 102s

Buy the right old building downtown, fix it up correctly, and the government hands you back close to half of what you spent. That is not a loophole and I am not being cute about it. The federal historic rehabilitation credit is twenty percent of your qualified rehab spending on a certified historic structure. Texas stacks its own on top, twenty-five percent, against state franchise tax.

Run both and you are looking at up to forty-five percent back on one project. The Texas side needs at least five thousand dollars of qualified spend, it is transferable, so you can sell it if you cannot use it, and it carries forward five years. There is also a reinvestment zone down here funding facade and preservation grants, which is a big part of why the Chadbourne corridor filled up with bars and restaurants instead of staying boarded. So why is nobody talking about this?

Because from the sidewalk these buildings look like nothing but problems. Old wiring, old plumbing, a roof somebody has been patching since the seventies. And honestly, for the wrong buyer, that is exactly what they are. But if you have a real use for the space and the patience to do the paperwork right, the math on a historic building beats anything new construction can put in front of you.

One catch, and it is a real one. The credits are unforgiving about how the work gets done, so you bring somebody in who has done this before you swing the first hammer.

12 Every branch of the military trains in this town Roosevelt Lofts 97s

Every single branch of the United States military trains in San Angelo. Air Force, Army, Marines, Navy, Space Force, all of them, on one installation on the edge of this town. Goodfellow puts about three point eight billion dollars into the Texas economy and supports somewhere around thirteen thousand jobs once you count everything downstream. The training group graduates close to fourteen thousand students a year, with about thirty-two hundred people on base on an average day.

Now, what does that do to a housing market? It does a lot, and most of us who grew up here quit noticing years ago because it has always been true. Here is the part I want you to hear if you served. Nearly ninety percent of VA backed home loans close with no money down at all.

None. And a VA loan never requires private mortgage insurance, which is a line most buyers pay every month for years and never see a dollar of again. There is a funding fee, and it moves depending on your down payment and whether this is your first time using it, so it is not free and I am not going to pretend it is. But if you are stationed here, or you got out and stayed, and you have been sitting on the sidelines because you think you need twenty percent in the bank, go find out what you actually qualify for.

You have been waiting for nothing.

13 Your tap water drives sixty-two miles to reach you Roosevelt Lofts 92s

Some of the water coming out of your tap in San Angelo did not come from anywhere near San Angelo. It gets pumped out of the Hickory Aquifer from a well field about fifteen miles south of Melvin, over in McCulloch County, and then it travels sixty-two miles through a pipeline to get here. And it comes up out of the ground carrying naturally occurring radium, so the city has to treat it and pull that out before it ever reaches a faucet. Did you know that?

Most people who have lived here their whole lives do not, and it is a genuinely impressive piece of infrastructure to have never thought about once. On top of that the city pulls from six different surface sources, Twin Buttes and O.C. Fisher and Nasworthy among them, because out here you do not get to depend on any single one. Now compare that to a well ten miles out of town, which is most likely pulling from the Edwards-Trinity, a completely different aquifer under a big stretch of West Texas. Same part of the state. Two totally different water stories, with two totally different sets of problems.

So when somebody tells you country water and city water are basically the same deal, they are not, and which one a house is on is worth knowing before you buy it instead of after.

14 Your school taxes just got cheaper Roosevelt Lofts 86s

Your school taxes went down and there is a decent chance nobody told you. The homestead exemption in Texas is the amount knocked off your home's value before your school taxes get calculated, and it is now a hundred and forty thousand dollars. Voters raised it again this past November. It was a hundred thousand before that, and forty thousand before that.

Three increases in three straight election cycles, which is exactly why so many people are still working off an old number. Sixty-five or older, or disabled? You get another sixty thousand on top, so you are at two hundred thousand exempted from school tax. And your city and your county can each add their own local exemption of up to twenty percent of appraised value on top of all of that. So what do you do with this?

Two things. If you bought a house recently and never filed for your homestead exemption, go file it. It is free, it takes a few minutes, and people forget constantly. Second, if somebody is quoting you a monthly payment on a house right now, make sure whoever ran that number used the current exemption and not last year's.

On a house in this market, that difference is real money every month you own the place.

15 Empty since 1983 and still looking Texas Theatre 74s

This was the biggest theater in West Texas. The Texas Theatre opened on Thanksgiving Day in 1929, two thousand seats, three hundred thousand dollars, drawn by an architect who built movie palaces all over the state. It closed in 1983. And it has been sitting right here ever since, carrying a local landmark designation, still listed as looking for a tenant.

Forty some years. So why has nobody taken it? Because a building like this needs two things to show up in the same person at the same time. A real use for two thousand seats, and the patience and the money to bring a 1929 structure up to what a modern occupancy code requires.

Those two things almost never arrive together, and that is the entire reason buildings like this sit. Now, empty is not the same as worthless. I want to be clear about that, because people look at this and see a teardown. Every downtown that ever turned itself around did it because somebody finally decided the hard building was worth the trouble.

This one is still waiting on that person.
