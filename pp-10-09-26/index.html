1
I sold tractors for twenty five years before I ever sold a ranch
The origin and the face
▾
Farmers and ranchers around San Angelo already know the Henderson name from the implement dealership. This script gives that recognition a face and ties it to the land he sells now.
Spot A
I sold tractors for twenty five years before I ever sold a ranch. I was born and raised in San Angelo. Grew up hunting and fishing all over this part of West Texas. Went to Angelo State, then went to work at Porter Henderson Implement. My granddad started that place in 1944. My brother and I ran it until we sold it to South Plains Implement in 2012.
Spot B
Here's what a job like that teaches you. Every farmer and rancher in the county comes through the door sooner or later. Usually because something is broken, and usually at a bad time. You learn what they run, what it costs them, and who they call first. You find out who is good for their word, and you find out fast who isn't. [OWNER: one dealership story, a rancher you helped on a bad day]
Spot C
These days my wife Genny and I raise cattle and sheep on our own place, down near Knickerbocker, in the southern part of Tom Green County. So when I walk a ranch with a buyer or a seller, I know what it takes to keep a place running through a dry summer. I'm Jeb Henderson with Pitcock Properties. If you bought a tractor or a baler from us over the years, you already know how I do business.
2
Knickerbocker was once the second biggest town in Tom Green County
The local anchor
▾
A piece of county history from the road he lives on, told by a man who lives on it. It is the kind of video a neighbor forwards with 'did you know this' attached, and it plants him as the guy who knows the ground.
Spot B
About eighteen miles west of San Angelo, where Farm Road 2335 meets Farm Road 584, there's a spot most folks drive right through. That's Knickerbocker, and I live down there. In the late 1890s it had two cotton gins, two hotels, two saloons, two blacksmith shops, and a sanatorium. About two hundred and fifty people. Only San Angelo had more size and political pull in this county.
Spot A
It got its name from two settlers who were related to Washington Irving. They named it for a character in his book about New York. That's how a sheep town in West Texas ends up with a name like that. Because it started with sheep. In 1877, a handful of men drove sheep into that valley and set up a ranch headquarters and a store. Two years before that, the Baze brothers dug an irrigation ditch to grow crops to sell at Fort Concho.
Spot C
Then in 1889 they moved the whole town south, just to reach a better water supply. Water has always decided where people live out here. Later the cars came, and the all weather roads, and the town got quiet. The last count I saw had it at ninety four people. I still look at that country the way those first settlers did. Where's the water, and what will this ground carry. That's the first thing I check on any piece of land.
3
Three questions to ask whoever sells you a ranch
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Sharpest of the four and the one that gets forwarded. It hands a buyer or seller a test any land agent should pass, and it lets his twenty five years around working ranchers do the talking without naming a soul.
Spot C
Before you hire anybody to buy or sell a ranch, ask them three questions. Have you ever run livestock on a place like this. Do you know what it carries in a dry year. And will you tell me what's wrong with it before I find out myself.
Spot A
A lot of folks selling land in this country never made a living off it. They know the comps and the software, and that matters. I use them too. But the photo in a listing got taken in a good year. It won't show you the tank that goes dry in August, or the fence line nobody has walked in ten years, or what that grass does when it doesn't rain.
Spot B
I raise cattle and sheep on my own place. I spent twenty five years selling equipment to people who do this for a living. When I walk a ranch with you, I look at the water, the fences and the grass, and I tell you what I see. A ranch is a huge investment, and it's going to shape your family's future. So ask the same three questions of anybody you're thinking of hiring, me included. Then go with the one who answers without blinking.
4
Four things to check on a ranch before you look at the house
The useful one
▾
Costs him nothing to give away and it is exactly what a first time land buyer does not know to ask. It earns the follow from every would-be ranch buyer in the county.
Spot A
If you're looking at land out here, look at the ground before the house. First thing is water. Is it a well, a creek, a tank, or a water line, and what does that source do when it hasn't rained in months. Next, access. A pretty place with no way in is a hard place to sell later. Is there a real road to the property, and is the right to use it written down somewhere.
Spot B
Third, the fences. Walk them if you can, because a fence tells you how the last owner ran the place. Who built them, who's kept them up, and do they sit where the survey says they do. A fence line can start a conversation with the neighbors you'd rather not have. Fourth, ask about the minerals. In Texas, who owns what's underground doesn't always go with the ground, so find out before you sign.
Spot C
Then ask what the place can carry. [OWNER: your rule of thumb for how many acres it takes to run a cow or a sheep around Tom Green County] That number tells you more than the view does. None of this takes a fancy tool. It takes a pickup, a pair of boots, and somebody who's done it. I'm Jeb Henderson with Pitcock Properties, and I'm happy to walk it with you.
One more idea
What a rancher learns at the parts counter

Stand at your own pickup or a pasture gate and tell one story about a customer who came in on a bad day and what you did about it. Open on the problem, end on how it got fixed. Ranchers who remember the dealership will recognize the moment.
