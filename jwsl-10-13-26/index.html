3
Ask the shop what they plan to do about the hole
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Most customers never learn that a worn bore can be repaired in place of replaced, and most shops never explain the choice. Laying out the question, and a customer's own three days versus one comparison, draws a fair line without naming anyone.
Spot C
When a piece of equipment has a worn bore, shops have a few answers. One answer is a new part. Another is a repair. Before you say yes to anything, ask the shop which one they're quoting. Then ask how long it will take, and what it costs. Ask whether they've worked on your kind of machine before. Ask what they'll measure first. Ask them to show you the bore measurements before and after. Ask whether the quote covers the bushings and pins too. Ask how long the machine will be down. Ask what you'll get in writing.
Spot A
With a repair, you weld up the worn hole and bore it back to the right size. A good line boring job leaves the hole round, the right size, and lined up with the hole on the other side. Your machine keeps the part it came with. [OWNER: how long a typical repair takes compared with waiting on a new part] [OWNER: your honest answer on price, a repair versus a new part]
Spot B
Here's what a customer wrote about us. Jobs that take others three days will take Justin one day, with much better quality. That came from a trucking company that has tried other shops. He said it better than I could. So ask the questions. Ask me, and ask the next guy. I'm happy to answer all of them.
4
Is it the bushing or the bore
The useful one
▾
Anyone running an excavator or loader has felt slop in a pivot and replaced the bushing, then felt it come back. Teaching the test costs nothing, sends the right jobs your way, and is exactly the knowledge a line boring shop is hired for.
Spot A
If a pivot on your machine has gotten sloppy, here's how to tell what's worn. Watch for knocking at the start and end of travel. Watch for a bucket that's lost its precision. Watch for movement that feels spongy under load. If you have a dial indicator, put it on the pivot with the machine shut down and the pivot unloaded. See how much it moves.
Spot B
Now look at the bushing. If it's loose but sits still, the wear is between the pin and the bushing. A new bushing probably handles that. If the bushing spins or hammers inside its seat, the bore itself is damaged. The hole has gone oval. You can put in a new bushing, and the play can come right back.
Spot C
A new bushing can hide a worn seat for a few weeks. Then it starts hammering again, and the second repair costs more, because the hole has worn further. Poor or dirty grease speeds all of this up. The earlier you catch a worn bore, the less the pin has chewed it up. So grease your pivots, and watch for play. [OWNER: the pivot you see worn out most often] If you can't tell whether it's the bushing or the bore, give me a call. Tell me what you're seeing, and I'll tell you what I think.
