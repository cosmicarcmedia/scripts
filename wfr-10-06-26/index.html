3
Ask for the numbers before anyone quotes you a foundation repair
Competitor contrast
▾
A homeowner has no yardstick when a stranger walks around the house with a clipboard. This script hands over the yardsticks the company publishes, plus its inspection fine print, so the viewer can test any contractor without a competitor being named.
Spot C
Before anybody quotes you a foundation repair, ask for the numbers. How far out of level is my house? The American Concrete Institute says a house should be no more than an inch and a half out of level. Past that, you're at risk in a natural disaster, or just from time passing. And how wide is that crack? Wider than a quarter of an inch, or running sideways, deserves a real look.
Spot A
Who's the person standing in your yard, and what are they certified in? I'm the only foundation repair specialist in West Texas certified by the National Foundation Repair Association. Then ask what the inspection costs. We inspect homes within ninety minutes of any of our offices. Commercial inspections are five hundred dollars. Past ninety minutes there may be a travel fee. And you should walk away with a clear explanation of any issues and the next steps.
Spot B
Ask what happens when it turns into an insurance claim. One customer with a slab leak wrote that we met with her insurance adjusters twice. And ask how fast they can start. A local realtor wrote that a house needed its foundation repaired before closing, within a few days, and that they'd never used us before.
4
Why that sticking door might be your foundation
Useful customer education
▾
Most homeowners cannot tell a settling house from a failing foundation, and the signs are free to give away. Everything here comes from the company's own service pages, and it earns the follow from anyone with a crack over the door.
Spot A
A door that sticks is easy to blame on an old house. Sometimes it's your foundation. When a foundation sinks, cracks, settles or heaves, it leaves you with an unlevel house, and it happens so slowly that a lot of homeowners never notice. Stabilizing a house may be one of the most important repairs it ever needs, because the safety and the life of your biggest investment depend on it.
Spot B
Look at your walls first. Cracks wider than a quarter of an inch matter, and so do cracks that run sideways. Check above your doors and windows, and the brick and mortar outside. Then check your floors. If they dip or feel softer than they used to, pay attention. A door or window frame that's gone uneven is a bigger problem than a sticky latch.
Spot C
Then walk the outside of the house. Rainwater and animals can wash the dirt out from under a slab and leave a void. Exposed tree roots, sagging fence posts and soil that's sunk away from the house are all clues. A plumbing leak under the house can move soil. So can poor drainage. If you see any of it, get the house measured. It should be no more than an inch and a half out of level.
