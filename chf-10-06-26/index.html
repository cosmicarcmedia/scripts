1
Third generation, from California to San Angelo
Origin
▾
Matt's own story is the strongest thing here, a third generation floor man who moved from California, started from scratch, and built a showroom floor that won an industry award, and it is on camera nowhere.
Spot A
My dad taught me how to install wood floors. His dad taught him. Now I'm teaching my son Carter. I'm Matt Garcia, and I'm a third generation floor contractor. I'm from California, and I moved to Texas and started from scratch. San Angelo is a long way from any big city, and I opened a showroom on Arroyo Street.
Spot B
When it came time to build the showroom floor, I wanted to show people what hardwood can do. I picked a hexagon pattern in walnut and white oak. Then I added a double helix border, which meant bending wood. I'd never done that before, and there was a lot of math and a lot of trial and error. Just taping off the white oak so I could stain the walnut took more than fourteen hours. That floor taught me that a slightly off angle becomes a big problem once the floor is down.
Spot C
That floor won Best in Parquetry in the 2021 Wood Floor of the Year awards. I still go to hardwood flooring events and take continuing courses. My installers train at NWFA facilities across the country, and I oversee their training myself. More than seventeen years in, that's still how I do it. I'm Matt Garcia, and this is Craftsman Hardwood Flooring on Arroyo Street.
2
The castle in West Texas, and the ranch's own rock and pecan
Local anchor
▾
A castle rising out of West Texas ranch country, with the ranch's own rock in the walls and pecan from its orchard in the floor, is a local story only Matt can tell, and he already wrote it up himself.
Spot B
There's a castle in West Texas, and my crew and I did the floors. It rises out of ranch country, and it was built to look like it has stood for centuries. The rock in the walls was quarried from the ranch. The pecan flooring in one room was milled from a pecan orchard on the property.
Spot A
So what do you put on the floor of a castle's great room? The owner's daughter is a designer, and her inspiration photos became the plan. We used seven inch wide engineered Texas post oak, woven around eighteen inch travertine tiles in a basketweave. It covers more than sixteen hundred square feet, in a room with rock walls. Wood and stone have very different performance requirements. The wood is three quarters of an inch thick and the travertine is a half inch, and the two had to meet perfectly flush.
Spot C
There was no manual for a floor like that, so we wrote the rules as we went. I told my crew, trust me, I have a plan. I could tell some days they wondered if I was a visionary or had finally lost my mind. We set about three hundred eighty travertine tiles, and only two had to be cut down. Wood Floor Business magazine ran the whole story, and I wrote it. It's a West Texas floor, and we're based right here in San Angelo.
3
Ask who's doing the work, and where they trained
Competitor contrast
▾
Matt's installers train at the national hardwood association and his sales staff study species and moisture there, so the question a homeowner should ask any flooring company comes straight from his own site.
Spot C
If you've ever had hardwood floors refinished, you know the drill. Seal off the rooms. Put the dog in the kennel. Take a day off work. We use the Atomic Dust Containment System by Bona, so those days are over.
Spot A
Who's doing the work, and where did they train? We send our installers to NWFA training facilities all over the country. I oversee that training myself. Our sales staff go through NWFA University, so they know species, moisture issues, grading, and underlayment. Our flooring comes from suppliers that specialize in wood flooring and other wood products.
Spot B
One customer wrote that they bought a house with 1980s parquet that had been carpeted over, with a wall built on top of it. They did the stripping. We repaired the damaged areas, matched the original pieces, and brought the floor into the 21st century. Another customer wrote that we followed up with tips on how to keep the floor looking good. A third wrote that Charlotte helped them pick the right flooring, and that I suggested a nice touch for their door frames. We'll measure your space, inspect the project, and give you a bid free of charge. A floor goes down once, so ask all of this first.
4
What your installer should check before the first board goes down
Useful customer education
▾
Matt's own castle write up shows what a pro checks first, the slab's moisture and its flatness, and handing that checklist to a homeowner for free is what makes the follow worth it.
Spot A
Subfloor prep is always the first priority. The first thing we do on a job is test the slab for moisture. We use moisture protection in the adhesive too. Then we check that the slab is flat. We lay a ten foot straightedge across it and mark the low spots to fill and the high spots to grind.
Spot B
On the castle floor, we got the slab flat to within an eighth of an inch over ten feet. Wood can take small variations. Stone has no give. At first I marked the grid lines with a tape measure, and after half the room we were half an inch off. We wiped every line off with a rag and started over with trammel points and a laser. That's why we checked, marked, and checked again before a single piece went down.
Spot C
If you're planning new floors, what should you ask your installer? How they test the slab for moisture. How they check it for flat. Whether the adhesive has moisture protection. Then ask what species fits your house. We walk people through solid versus engineered, and which one holds up in their environment and lifestyle. It costs nothing to ask, and a good installer will have answers.
