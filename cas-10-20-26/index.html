3
Ask how your kayak was made
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Our own Kayak FAQ already explains the three ways kayaks get built and says we carry only roto molded ones, so this turns it into one question a customer can ask at any counter.
Spot C
Here's a question to ask before you buy any kayak. How was it made? There are three main ways. Roto molding pours plastic resin into a hollow mold and spins it in an oven on two axes. You get one solid piece with an even wall thickness. It's very durable, and it costs more to make, because every model needs its own mold, plus time for the cooking and the cooling. Many of the top kayak makers build that way. We only carry roto molded kayaks.
Spot A
Thermoforming heats a sheet of plastic over a mold and pulls it tight with a vacuum. Two pieces get welded along the middle. Those kayaks tend to be lighter and often cost less, and the quality depends on the maker. Blow molding blows hot air into plastic like a balloon and stamps it into shape. It's the quickest way to build one, and the designs tend to be simpler.
Spot B
Ask how it was made. Ask how long it will last. [OWNER: the longest you have seen a roto molded kayak last] We've been in the kayak business for many years. Prices here run from around five hundred dollars into the thousands, so there's room to start small. Ask the same questions anywhere you shop, then come put your hand on one here and ask us.
4
How to pick a kayak by what you plan to do with it
The useful one
▾
This is the question the shop gets all the time, answered the way its own Kayak FAQ answers it, with the same Corvette and semi comparison, so a first-time buyer leaves knowing what to look for.
Spot A
People ask us all the time which kayak is the best. It depends on what you value most. Someone who wants exercise needs something different from someone fishing a tournament. Figure out what you want to do first.
Spot B
Then, sit on top or sit inside? Sit on top kayaks are wider and feel more stable, so they're easiest for new paddlers. Sit inside kayaks put you lower in the boat, which gives more control in moving water, but they tend to feel tippy. Your first feeling on the water is called primary stability. How far the boat leans before it tips is secondary stability. Then length. Shorter kayaks turn better and are easier to store and haul, but wind and current push them around. Longer ones track better, so you paddle less to hold your line, and they carry more.
Spot C
Think of a Corvette and a semi. A Corvette speeds up fast and takes corners. A semi takes longer to do everything and needs more room to turn. Price works the same way. A Honda Civic and an F350 King Ranch both get you from A to B, at two different prices, with two different jobs. [OWNER: your own pick for a first kayak] Tell us what you want to do, and we'll point you to the right one. Happy adventures.
