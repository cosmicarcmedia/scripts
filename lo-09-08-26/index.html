    If you’ve been on testosterone for any amount of time, there’s a pretty good chance somebody has told you this:

    “Make sure you donate blood.”

    Or maybe your labs came back and your hematocrit was 52, 53, 54 percent, and somebody immediately told you:

    “Your blood is getting too thick. You need to donate before you have a stroke.”

    And I want to talk about that, because there is some legitimate physiology buried inside that advice.

    Testosterone absolutely can increase your red blood cell production.

    Testosterone absolutely can raise your hemoglobin and hematocrit.

    And an excessively elevated hematocrit is not something that I ignore.

    But somewhere along the way we took all of that and turned it into:

    *testosterone equals thick blood, thick blood equals blood clots, therefore everybody on testosterone needs to donate blood.*

    And that is much too simplistic.

    In fact, if we repeatedly bleed somebody every time their hematocrit creeps up without ever asking *why* it’s elevated, we can create an entirely different problem: *iron deficiency.*

    So today we’re going deep into this.

    We’re going to talk about what hematocrit actually is.

    Why testosterone raises it.

    Where this famous 54-percent number comes into the conversation.

    Why testosterone-related erythrocytosis is not the same thing as polycythemia vera.

    What the testosterone studies actually show about blood clots.

    What we do when someone’s hematocrit starts climbing.

    And then we’re going to get into ferritin, iron and a hormone called hepcidin, because this is the part of this conversation that I think gets missed more than almost anything else.

    And by the end I want you to understand one thing:

    *High hematocrit deserves respect. It does not deserve panic.* ------------------------------ *PART 1 — WHAT IS HEMATOCRIT?*

    First, hematocrit is not some mysterious clotting test.

    It is simply the percentage of your blood volume that’s occupied by red blood cells.

    So if your hematocrit is 45 percent, roughly 45 percent of your circulating blood volume consists of red cells.

    And immediately, that tells you something important:

    *Hematocrit depends on both the numerator and the denominator.*

    The numerator is your red blood cell volume.

    The denominator includes your plasma volume.

    That means you can raise hematocrit in two fundamentally different ways.

    You can genuinely increase your red blood cell mass.

    Or you can decrease your plasma volume.

    That’s why somebody who is significantly dehydrated can show up with an elevated hematocrit without suddenly manufacturing a bunch of new red blood cells overnight.

    The red cells stayed the same.

    The liquid around them decreased.

    So when I see an unexpectedly high hematocrit, context matters.

    Was this patient fasting?

    Had he been outside working in West Texas all day?

    Has he been sweating?

    Is he dehydrated?

    Or is this a reproducible elevation that has been climbing over several months?

    Those aren’t the same problem.

    [ON SCREEN: *Hematocrit = RBC volume ÷ total blood volume*] ------------------------------ *PART 2 — WHY TESTOSTERONE RAISES HEMATOCRIT*

    Testosterone really does stimulate red blood cell production.

    This isn’t controversial.

    Testosterone affects erythropoietin, or EPO, which is one of the main hormonal signals telling the bone marrow to make red blood cells.

    It also appears to establish a higher EPO-to-hemoglobin set point.

    And testosterone suppresses a hormone called *hepcidin*, which we’ll come back to later because it’s incredibly important for iron.

    The end result is that testosterone makes iron more available to the bone marrow and encourages erythropoiesis — the production of new red blood cells.

    And the effect is dose and exposure dependent.

    The Endocrine Society specifically notes that increases in hematocrit with testosterone are related to testosterone dose and circulating concentrations.

    This is also why testosterone is actually capable of *correcting anemia* in some hypogonadal men.

    We’re talking about the same biological effect.

    If someone’s hemoglobin is 11.5 and testosterone increases it to 14, we call that a treatment benefit.

    If someone’s hemoglobin starts at 16 and hematocrit eventually reaches 55 percent, now we’re calling that erythrocytosis.

    Same pathway.

    Different starting point and different magnitude. ------------------------------ *PART 3 — STOP CALLING EVERYTHING “POLYCYTHEMIA”*

    This is where terminology causes a ton of unnecessary fear.

    A guy on testosterone develops a hematocrit of 54 percent and somebody tells him:

    “You have polycythemia.”

    What they usually mean is:

    *you have erythrocytosis.*

    You have an increased concentration or mass of red blood cells.

    But then that gets mentally lumped together with:

    *polycythemia vera.*

    And polycythemia vera is an entirely different disease.

    Polycythemia vera is a *clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm.*

    Essentially, we’re dealing with an abnormal bone-marrow clone, most commonly involving the JAK2 pathway.

    And the thrombotic biology in PV is not simply:

    “Too many red blood cells made the blood thick.”

    PV can involve abnormal red-cell behavior, activated platelets, activated leukocytes, inflammation, endothelial activation, abnormal adhesion between blood cells and the vessel wall, and the JAK2 mutation itself appears to participate in this prothrombotic environment.

    So yes, hematocrit matters enormously in PV.

    But it matters inside a disease that is already fundamentally prothrombotic.

    That’s very different from saying:

    “A healthy man developed secondary erythrocytosis because testosterone stimulated his marrow, therefore he has the clotting biology of somebody with polycythemia vera.”

    He doesn’t. ------------------------------ *PART 4 — WHY EVERYONE IS SO AGGRESSIVE WITH HEMATOCRIT IN PV*

    There is a great study called CYTO-PV.

    Researchers took patients who actually had polycythemia vera and compared tighter versus looser hematocrit control.

    One group was maintained below 45 percent.

    The other was maintained between 45 and 50 percent.

    Major cardiovascular death or thrombosis occurred in about:

    *2.7 percent* of the tighter-control group,

    versus

    *9.8 percent* of the 45-to-50-percent group.

    That is a huge difference.

    And therefore, if you’re a hematologist treating somebody with polycythemia vera, you take hematocrit extremely seriously.

    You should.

    But here’s the critical sentence:

    *That trial studied polycythemia vera.*

    It did not study testosterone patients.

    It did not show that a 49-percent hematocrit in a testosterone patient carries the same risk as a 49-percent hematocrit in somebody with JAK2-positive PV.

    We cannot transplant the treatment strategy from one disease into another without considering why that hematocrit is elevated. ------------------------------ *PART 5 — ALTITUDE IS A GREAT EXAMPLE*

    Here’s a useful natural experiment.

    Healthy men who permanently live at high altitude have higher hematocrits.

    At about 4,000 meters — roughly 13,000 feet — researchers studied almost 2,000 healthy young residents in Bolivia.

    The average hematocrit in the men was:

    *52.7 percent.*

    And their calculated normal range extended from approximately:

    *45 to 61 percent.*

    So a hematocrit of 55 percent cannot possibly be a universal biological switch where the blood suddenly becomes pathologically thrombogenic.

    Otherwise a massive percentage of healthy high-altitude populations would be walking medical emergencies.

    Now, please don’t mishear me.

    *I’m not saying a testosterone patient at sea level should have a hematocrit of 61 percent.*

    I’m saying the reason for the erythrocytosis matters.

    Altitude causes secondary erythrocytosis predominantly because hypoxia stimulates EPO and erythropoiesis.

    Testosterone causes secondary erythrocytosis through somewhat different signaling involving EPO, hepcidin and marrow stimulation.

    Neither one is identical to polycythemia vera.

    A hematology review made this point beautifully: thrombosis does *not* accompany most forms of erythrocytosis, which tells us hematocrit itself cannot be the entire thrombotic story.

    There’s even recent work in Andean highlanders with extreme erythrocytosis that did not demonstrate the expected hypercoagulable state on sophisticated clot-testing methods. That does not prove extreme hematocrit is harmless, but it reinforces that *viscosity and thrombogenicity are not synonymous.*

    And even hematology guidelines treat secondary hypoxic erythrocytosis differently from PV.

    The British Society for Haematology suggests considering venesection in hypoxic pulmonary disease when patients have hyperviscosity symptoms or hematocrit above about *56 percent*, and even then they suggest reducing it toward *50 to 52 percent*, not down below 45 like PV.

    Same lab.

    Different biology.

    Different treatment.

    That’s important. ------------------------------ *PART 6 — SO WHERE DOES 54 PERCENT COME IN?*

    The major testosterone guidelines have settled around a hematocrit of *54 percent* as an intervention point.

    The AUA says that during testosterone therapy, a hematocrit of 54 percent or greater warrants intervention, with dose adjustment as first-line management when on-treatment testosterone is high.

    The Endocrine Society similarly treats hematocrit above 54 percent as erythrocytosis requiring action.

    But here’s a sentence directly reflected in the Endocrine Society guideline:

    *The hematocrit level at which the risk of cardiovascular or neuro-occlusive events increases is not known.*

    That is critically important.

    So 54 percent is: *an action threshold.*

    It is *not*: *a proven thrombosis cliff.*

    There wasn’t a randomized testosterone trial where they discovered:

    53.9 — everything’s great.

    54.0 — everybody starts clotting.

    That’s not what happened.

    So when I see a 54-percent hematocrit, I take action.

    But my definition of *action* is not automatically:

    “Go dump a pint of blood.” ------------------------------ *PART 7 — DOES TESTOSTERONE CAUSE BLOOD CLOTS?*

    This needs nuance, because both sides of this argument tend to become too absolute.

    I would not tell you:

    “Testosterone cannot cause a blood clot.”

    That’s stronger than our evidence supports.

    But I also would not tell you that appropriately prescribed testosterone has been demonstrated to create some enormous thrombotic risk.

    Randomized-trial meta-analyses overall have *not demonstrated a statistically significant increase in venous thromboembolism with TRT*, although the confidence intervals remain wide enough that a modest increase cannot be completely excluded.

    And then we have TRAVERSE.

    This was the biggest cardiovascular safety trial of testosterone that we’ve had.

    More than 5,000 men with hypogonadism who either already had cardiovascular disease or were at increased cardiovascular risk were randomized to transdermal testosterone gel or placebo.

    Major cardiovascular events occurred in:

    *7.0 percent* on testosterone

    versus

    *7.3 percent* on placebo.

    Testosterone was noninferior to placebo for the primary cardiovascular endpoint.

    That’s very reassuring.

    But I don’t want to cherry-pick the study.

    Overall VTE occurred in:

    *1.7 percent on testosterone*

    versus

    *1.2 percent on placebo.*

    The hazard ratio was 1.46, but the confidence interval crossed one, so this difference was not statistically significant.

    Pulmonary embolism specifically occurred in:

    *0.9 versus 0.5 percent.*

    DVT was:

    *0.6 versus 0.5 percent.*

    So there was a PE signal.

    We should acknowledge it.

    TRAVERSE occurred during the COVID era, but subsequent analysis did *not establish that COVID simply explains away the PE finding*, so I would not use COVID as an excuse to dismiss it.

    And there’s another limitation that’s hugely relevant to today’s conversation.

    TRAVERSE does *not* prove it’s safe to leave testosterone patients at hematocrits of 56 or 58 percent.

    Why?

    Because patients with a high baseline hematocrit were excluded, testosterone doses were reduced if Hct exceeded 54 percent, and treatment was discontinued if Hct remained above 54 despite reducing to the lowest dose.

    So the trial simply didn’t test that strategy. ------------------------------ *PART 8 — WHAT ABOUT MEN WHO ACTUALLY DEVELOP HIGH HEMATOCRIT?*

    This is the data point I think we need to respect.

    A large retrospective database study compared testosterone users who developed hematocrit of at least 52 percent with testosterone users who didn’t.

    MACE or VTE occurred in:

    *5.15 percent*

    versus

    *3.87 percent.*

    That was an odds ratio of about *1.35.*

    So there is an observational signal.

    And therefore my message isn’t:

    “Hematocrit doesn’t matter.”

    My message is:

    *We don’t know that hematocrit itself is the entire causal mechanism.*

    The man who develops erythrocytosis may be different in other ways.

    He may have:

    sleep apnea,

    smoking,

    obesity,

    hypoxia,

    higher testosterone exposure,

    different pharmacokinetics,

    higher baseline hematocrit,

    or other cardiovascular risk factors.

    And most importantly:

    We do *not* have a randomized trial showing that repeatedly phlebotomizing testosterone patients to a particular Hct prevents those cardiovascular events.

    A 2024 review specifically examined this issue and concluded that evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of routine phlebotomy for testosterone-induced erythrocytosis is lacking.

    That’s where I think the conversation needs to change. ------------------------------ *PART 9 — WHAT WE ACTUALLY DO WHEN HEMATOCRIT RISES*

    Here’s our general approach.

    This is not me telling you how to personally manage your testosterone over the internet.

    This is how I think through the physiology as a clinician. *Baseline*

    If someone walks into my office *before ever starting testosterone* with a hematocrit of 54 percent, I don’t say:

    “Cool. Let’s add testosterone.”

    I want to know why.

    The AUA actually recommends considering withholding testosterone even above a baseline hematocrit of 50 percent until the cause is understood.

    At sea level, unexplained baseline erythrocytosis deserves an explanation.

    Could it be dehydration?

    Smoking?

    Sleep-disordered breathing?

    Pulmonary disease?

    Altitude?

    Another medication?

    An EPO-producing process?

    A primary hematologic disorder?

    We need context. ------------------------------ *Hct around 50 to 53.9 percent on treatment*

    I’m usually looking at the *trend*.

    A man who has lived at:

    50.5,

    50.8,

    51.1,

    50.7,

    for years,

    is different from somebody who went:

    47,

    50,

    52,

    53.8,

    in six months.

    I’m looking at the patient, not just the number. ------------------------------ *Hct 54 to 57.9 percent*

    This is where I think *some type of protocol intervention is warranted.*

    Not necessarily phlebotomy.

    Intervention.

    First, I look for the obvious contributors. *Sleep apnea.*

    This is huge.

    In one TRT cohort, obstructive sleep apnea was independently associated with developing hematocrit of 52 percent or greater, with roughly doubled odds after adjustment.

    So the guy who has:

    BMI 37,

    snores like a chainsaw,

    wakes up exhausted,

    has hypertension,

    and suddenly develops a 55-percent hematocrit?

    I’m much more interested in what his oxygen saturation does from midnight to six in the morning than I am in locating the nearest blood bank. *Smoking.*

    Including the possibility of elevated carboxyhemoglobin. *Hydration.*

    But let’s be clear about hydration:

    Hydration can fix *hemoconcentration*.

    It cannot magically eliminate excessive red-cell mass.

    If somebody has a real RBC-mass problem, drinking another gallon of water isn’t treatment. *Testosterone exposure.*

    What’s the dose?

    What’s the free testosterone?

    Are we running somebody unnecessarily high?

    If a guy has a free testosterone through the roof and Hct of 56, my first thought is not exotic hematology.

    My first thought is:

    *maybe we’re giving him too much testosterone.* ------------------------------ *PART 10 — CHANGE THE DELIVERY WHEN APPROPRIATE*

    Injection pattern matters.

    One study compared:

    100 milligrams IM weekly

    versus

    200 milligrams every other week.

    Hematocrit above 54 percent occurred in:

    about *1 percent* of the weekly group

    versus

    *8 percent* of the every-other-week group.

    That supports the concept that large intermittent exposure and peaks can be more erythrogenic.

    Does that prove that splitting every patient’s weekly dose into twice-weekly or three-times-weekly injections will fix hematocrit?

    No.

    We don’t have that trial.

    But trying to smooth testosterone exposure is physiologically reasonable.

    Subcutaneous therapy may help in some patients as well, although the evidence isn’t clean enough to say that simply taking the exact same cypionate dose from IM to SQ automatically fixes hematocrit.

    One comparison found lower post-treatment hematocrit with a subcutaneous testosterone enanthate autoinjector versus weekly IM cypionate — encouraging, but it changed formulation and delivery, not just injection depth.

    And then there’s transdermal testosterone.

    This is probably the strongest formulation change if hematocrit continues to be a problem.

    In one comparative study, erythrocytosis was much more common with injectable testosterone than testosterone gel.

    So for the guy who genuinely feels better on testosterone but repeatedly runs excessive hematocrit on injections:

    *cream or gel can be an excellent tool.* ------------------------------ *PART 11 — CARDIO AND HYDRATION*

    I absolutely want these guys exercising.

    Partly because if we’re talking about cardiovascular risk, improving cardiovascular fitness is a pretty good place to start.

    Regular endurance training tends to expand plasma volume.

    And because hematocrit is a ratio, increasing plasma volume can reduce measured hematocrit.

    There’s even a small interventional study in high-altitude residents with excessive erythrocytosis where eight weeks of moderate aerobic training reduced hematocrit, and the investigators concluded most of that reduction appeared to come from *plasma-volume expansion rather than destruction of RBC mass.*

    I think that’s fascinating.

    Does that prove Zone 2 treats testosterone erythrocytosis?

    No.

    So I don’t prescribe cardio as hematology treatment.

    I prescribe cardio because it improves cardiovascular health and because expanding plasma volume may improve the measured hematocrit as a secondary benefit. ------------------------------ *PART 12 — WHEN DO I GET MUCH MORE AGGRESSIVE?*

    As you start approaching a confirmed hematocrit of *58 percent or higher*, my tolerance for watchful waiting becomes very low.

    At that point I’m seriously reducing or holding testosterone exposure.

    I’m looking harder for secondary disease.

    I’m considering sleep testing.

    I’m thinking about oxygenation and pulmonary disease.

    I’m looking at whether anything in the rest of the CBC makes this look like something other than testosterone.

    And yes:

    *phlebotomy can absolutely become appropriate.*

    I’m not anti-phlebotomy.

    I’m anti-*reflexive chronic phlebotomy without fixing the problem.*

    Those are completely different positions.

    If somebody is symptomatic with concerning neurologic, cardiopulmonary or thrombotic symptoms, obviously the urgency changes regardless of some arbitrary cutoff.

    And if erythrocytosis persists despite fixing the obvious secondary causes and reducing testosterone exposure, that’s when additional testing — potentially EPO, JAK2 testing or hematology involvement — becomes much more appropriate. ------------------------------ *PART 13 — NOW LET’S TALK ABOUT THE DOWNSIDE OF BLOOD DONATION*

    This is where this entire video changes direction.

    Because blood donation works beautifully for lowering hematocrit.

    You remove red blood cells.

    Hematocrit falls.

    Done.

    Except…

    You didn’t only remove red blood cells.

    You also removed a huge amount of *iron.*

    A typical whole-blood donation removes roughly: *200 to 250 milligrams of iron.*

    That’s not insignificant.

    Your body then has to replace the red blood cells you just removed.

    And what does your bone marrow need to make those red blood cells?

    *Iron.*

    Where does it get that iron?

    Your iron stores.

    So now imagine somebody on testosterone.

    Testosterone is stimulating erythropoiesis.

    Their Hct rises.

    Someone tells them to donate.

    They lose 200-plus milligrams of iron.

    Their body rebuilds those RBCs.

    Their marrow consumes more iron.

    Hct rises again.

    They donate again.

    And they repeat this cycle every eight weeks.

    Eventually the patient can wind up with:

    a perfectly decent hemoglobin,

    a still relatively high hematocrit,

    and absolutely garbage iron stores. ------------------------------ *PART 14 — FERRITIN*

    Ferritin is basically our iron-storage marker.

    The analogy I use with my providers is: *Ferritin is your savings account.*

    It tells me how much iron you’ve got sitting in reserve.

    And then I look at: *transferrin saturation — TSAT.*

    That’s more like your checking account.

    It tells me how much circulating iron is readily available.

    You can have a hemoglobin of 16 and still have depleted iron stores.

    That is a very important point.

    A normal CBC does *not* rule out iron deficiency.

    Frequent blood donors are a perfect example of this. Hemoglobin screening alone can miss iron deficiency without anemia.

    And this is one of the reasons we’ve added baseline ferritin in our testosterone patients.

    Because if somebody starts at:

    *ferritin 110*

    and two years later they’re:

    *ferritin 14*

    after six blood donations,

    that’s extremely useful information. ------------------------------ *PART 15 — WHY IRON DEFICIENCY CAN MAKE YOU FEEL BAD EVEN BEFORE ANEMIA*

    People tend to think:

    “Iron only matters because iron makes hemoglobin.”

    That’s not true.

    Iron is involved in mitochondrial energy production and multiple oxidative enzymes.

    So iron deficiency can potentially affect exercise performance and cellular energy metabolism even before someone becomes frankly anemic.

    The athlete literature on iron deficiency without anemia is not perfectly consistent, but there is evidence that low iron status can impair performance in at least some iron-deficient athletes, and the biological rationale is strong.

    Clinically, some of the things that get my attention are:

    fatigue,

    restless legs,

    brain fog,

    poor exercise tolerance,

    and especially somebody saying:

    “Zone 2 is okay, but whenever I try to push hard and sustain it, something just feels off.”

    Does low ferritin automatically explain every one of those symptoms?

    No.

    But if somebody has ferritin 15 and TSAT 10 percent, I’m not ignoring that because his hemoglobin happens to be 16. ------------------------------ *PART 16 — NOW HEPCIDIN MAKES THE WHOLE THING MAKE SENSE*

    Hepcidin is probably the most important hormone you’ve never heard about when it comes to iron.

    Hepcidin is primarily produced by the liver.

    Think of it as the *gatekeeper for iron.*

    Or if you prefer:

    the bouncer.

    When hepcidin is high:

    *the iron gates close.*

    You absorb less iron from your intestines.

    You release less recycled iron from macrophages.

    You release less stored iron into circulation.

    When hepcidin is low:

    *the gates open.*

    More iron gets absorbed.

    More stored and recycled iron becomes available.

    The primary protein hepcidin controls is called *ferroportin*, which is basically the iron-export door on these cells.

    Now here’s where testosterone comes back into this.

    Testosterone suppresses hepcidin.

    In a controlled testosterone study, higher doses markedly suppressed hepcidin within *one week*, and the hepcidin suppression was associated with the subsequent rise in hematocrit.

    So testosterone is essentially telling the body:

    *Open the iron gates. We’re building red blood cells.*

    And that’s part of why you can see:

    hematocrit go up,

    while ferritin comes down.

    Iron is being pulled out of storage and utilized by the bone marrow. ------------------------------ *PART 17 — NOW ADD PHLEBOTOMY*

    So imagine that system again.

    Testosterone:

    increases erythropoiesis,

    suppresses hepcidin,

    mobilizes iron.

    Then you remove a pint of blood.

    The body says:

    “We just lost red blood cells.”

    Now erythropoietic demand goes up again.

    And you also just removed 200 to 250 milligrams of iron.

    So your body pulls more iron out of storage to rebuild those cells.

    Do that repeatedly and you can create a patient whose marrow keeps successfully maintaining hemoglobin while their ferritin gets progressively wiped out.

    That’s the patient I don’t want us creating. ------------------------------ *PART 18 — THE RIDICULOUS CYCLE*

    Here’s the guy I don’t want.

    He starts:

    Hct 47.

    Ferritin 120.

    He starts testosterone.

    Hct goes to 54.

    Someone says:

    “Donate.”

    Now he’s 49.

    Great.

    Two months later he’s 52.

    “Donate again.”

    Now he’s 48.

    Then 52.

    Donate.

    Then 51.

    Donate.

    Nobody ever checked ferritin.

    A year later:

    Hematocrit 51.

    Hemoglobin 16.

    Ferritin *8.*

    TSAT *9 percent.*

    He’s exhausted.

    He can’t train like he used to.

    His legs are restless.

    And somebody looks at his CBC and says:

    “Well, your hematocrit is still 51. You’d probably better donate.”

    At some point:

    *we need to stop treating a number and start treating the physiology.* ------------------------------ *PART 19 — ORAL IRON AND HEPCIDIN*

    There’s one other cool piece of hepcidin physiology.

    Taking oral iron itself temporarily raises hepcidin.

    In human absorption studies, an oral dose of about 60 milligrams or greater increased hepcidin for roughly the next 24 hours.

    And that reduced fractional absorption of the next iron dose by approximately *35 to 45 percent.*

    That’s why the old-school:

    iron morning,

    iron noon,

    iron night,

    strategy isn’t necessarily the most efficient approach.

    The first iron dose partially closes the door on the next one.

    That’s where once-daily or alternate-day dosing strategies came from.

    Alternate-day dosing can improve fractional absorption and often tolerability, although clinical trials have not shown that alternate-day dosing is universally superior to daily dosing for every endpoint.

    So again:

    physiology matters more than simply saying:

    “Ferritin is low. Take as much iron as humanly possible.” ------------------------------ *PART 20 — WHAT WE DO WITH LOW FERRITIN*

    At our clinic, this is why we now obtain baseline ferritin.

    If somebody develops low ferritin, especially with low transferrin saturation, I want to know *why*.

    If they’ve donated blood six times?

    Pretty easy explanation.

    If they’re a male who’s never donated blood and suddenly has genuine iron deficiency?

    That’s a completely different conversation.

    Where is the iron going?

    GI blood loss?

    Malabsorption?

    NSAID use?

    Celiac disease?

    Another GI process?

    If a man has unexplained iron deficiency or iron-deficiency anemia with no obvious blood-donation history, that’s where GI evaluation becomes important.

    Don’t just cover unexplained male iron deficiency with supplements forever.

    For somebody whose deficiency clearly appears to be donation-associated and whose hematocrit is safely controlled, oral iron replacement can make sense.

    Our preferred oral approach is something tolerable like *ferrous bisglycinate*, usually using a meaningful single dose rather than divided doses throughout the day.

    Then we follow:

    CBC,

    ferritin,

    and often TSAT.

    Because we need to see both sides of the equation.

    Are the iron stores recovering?

    And what is the hematocrit doing as that iron becomes available again? ------------------------------ *PART 21 — THIS IS WHY PHLEBOTOMY CAN BECOME SELF-DEFEATING*

    Phlebotomy is extraordinarily effective at lowering hematocrit in the moment.

    But if your long-term strategy is:

    *keep testosterone exposure identical*

    plus
