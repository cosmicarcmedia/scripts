At The Yard

Rural Waste Management · Thursday, September 24, 2026 · 15 scripts

Fifteen scripts for Thursday, all shot at the yard across four stops. Your six ideas are the first seven of them. Every one runs about a minute and a half. You are not memorizing these. They go on the iPad and you read them, and we do as many takes as it takes to sound like you instead of like a page.

Read them out loud once before Thursday if you get the chance. Anywhere a line does not sound like something you would actually say, change it. The facts need to stay put, the wording does not. Scripts 1 and 2 are demos, so we need the carts and whatever dumpster sizes you can stage sitting out before we roll.

The scripts

1 How many carts do you actually need Containers staged 84s

I get asked this every single week. How many carts do I need, or should I just get a dumpster? Almost everybody guesses wrong, and they all guess the same direction. They go too small.

This is a ninety five gallon cart. Let me just show you what fits in one. People are usually looking at the wrong number. A family of four, normal week, nothing unusual going on, that's about one cart.

Put a couple of kids in diapers in that house and you're at two. That's part of why we let you have up to two of them. Where folks get caught out is a project. You clean out a garage on a Saturday and the cart's full before you've reached the back wall.

That's the week you wanted a dumpster. We run three dumpster sizes. A yard and a half, three yard, and four yard. Same sizes and the same price whether you're a house or a business, because we don't split those up.

You ought to be paying for what you actually put out. So don't guess. Count your bags for one week and call me with that number. Thirty six ninety nine a month starts it in Tom Green County, all in, tax included.

2 Where your cart sits changes everything Containers staged 76s

Our truck is a side loader, which means an arm comes out and grabs your cart. That arm needs room. Not a lot of room, but some. When it doesn't have it, that's usually the call that comes in saying we skipped somebody.

Wheels toward the house, handle toward the house, lid opening away from the street. Give it about six feet on either side. Six feet from the mailbox, six feet from the parked car, six feet from the other cart if you've got two. Keep it out from under the low branches.

And leave the lid closed, because a lid standing up is the thing that catches. None of that is us being picky, it's just what the arm can reach. Same idea with a dumpster. The truck has to pull straight up to it.

If you've parked a trailer in front of it, or somebody blocked it in overnight, we can't get it. And anything sticking up out of the top, we can't pick that up either. Want the easiest version of all this? Put it where you'd want to walk past it, then take one step back.

3 What happens if you forget to roll it out Containers staged 66s

Somebody asked us this on Facebook and I don't think we've ever answered it out loud. If they forget to put the cart out, is there an extra charge? No. There's no charge, there's no fee, there's no note on your account.

You forgot. Everybody forgets. I've forgotten, and I own the company. Your trash sits until the next week and then we take it, and that's the whole story.

I know why people ask, though. Somewhere along the way a trash company charged them for something that felt like nothing. A missed cart, a late payment by two days, a fuel charge that showed up out of nowhere. So now everybody's braced for it.

We'd rather you call us than guess. Three two five, seven one zero, six two two two. A person picks that up. And if we're the ones who missed you, we come back and get it for free, which is a different thing entirely and also not a fee.

4 They save the best price for strangers Office door 76s

I want to talk about something in this industry that I think is genuinely insane. The big companies run sign up specials. Low introductory rate, first few months half off, that kind of thing. So who gets that price?

Strangers. Not the family that's been paying them every month for six years without one late payment. That family's at the top of the price list. The guy who just moved in next door to them gets it at half.

And the loyal customer finds out when they're standing in the driveway talking to the new neighbor about trash of all things. Think about what that says about how a company sees you. You're worth the most to them on the day you sign and a little bit less every month after. Then there's the other half of it.

That great rate, is it locked in, or did you just sign something? Because a lot of these outfits stopped calling it a contract. Now it's a service agreement. Same paper, friendlier word.

Everybody here pays the same thing, and the number I quote you is the number on the bill.

5 Where does your trash money actually go Office door 68s

Here's a question nobody thinks to ask about their trash bill. Where does that money end up? One of the big ones serving out here answers to a headquarters in Phoenix, Arizona. Another one runs out of a town south of Austin.

Another one's up in Seminole. Those are real companies doing real work, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But your money gets on the road the same day your trash does. It just doesn't come back.

So think about what that means. Where do they buy their supplies? Where do they pay their property taxes? Which school district sees that money?

Whose fuel stop do those trucks use? Which body shop do they call when something gets backed into? Every one of those answers is a town that isn't yours. We buy our dumpsters, pay our taxes, fuel our trucks and send our kids to school inside the same fifteen communities we pick up in.

Your bill doesn't leave the county. That's just where the bank is.

6 All the perks and none of the bull Office door 78s

Let me just lay the differences out plain and you can decide. We give you an honest quote up front, and it's the whole number. No hidden fees. No fuel surcharge that appears in month four.

No contract, and no contract wearing a new name either. No cancellation fee if you want to leave. And no price increase that shows up with no explanation attached to it. That last one matters more than people realize.

When was the last time a company told you why a price went up? We've raised prices three times. Every single time, everybody got a full month of notice and a reason in plain English. We lost one customer across all three.

One. Turns out if you tell people why, most of them are fine. The other thing we don't do is the cheap starter rate. You know the one.

Twenty four dollars to get you signed, and by month four you're somewhere north of fifty and wondering how. We'd rather quote you the real number on day one and still be here in three years. All the perks and none of the bull. That's about the size of it.

7 Part of your bill stays in this county Office door 74s

Does your trash company give back to the place it picks up in? That sounds like a soft question until you actually go looking for the answer. Most people have never checked. It's trash service, so why would you.

We've sponsored charity events out here. Little league teams. Cheer squads. Ad slots in school programs, and school events that needed somebody to cover a cost nobody had budgeted.

Programs that needed a banner and had nobody to buy one. I'm not going to sit here and read the whole list at you, but it's longer than you'd think for a company our size. And I want to be careful how I say this next part. We don't do that because it polls well.

My wife and I have four adopted kids, so the youth programs around here hit us somewhere specific. It's just what happens when the owners live where the trucks run. So part of what you pay every month ends up back in your own community. More customers means we can do more of it.

That's the whole pitch, and it's a real one.

8 I ran a curbing company before this Truck line 77s

Everybody around here knows I was a state trooper and now I run a trash company. What nobody knows is there's a whole business in between those two. I turned in my badge after six years. And then I went and started a decorative concrete curbing company.

I'd never even heard of curbing before I started doing it. That's the part I'd want somebody to hear. I didn't leave law enforcement with a plan. I left it with a family to feed and a thing I'd found that looked like it might work.

So I learned it. Ran it a couple of years, maybe closer to three. Traveled for it, worked all over the state doing it. And then I found something I liked better and did the same thing again.

Learned that one from nothing too. Which is how you end up standing in front of a line of trucks you didn't own three years ago. Does any of that sound like a guy with a career plan? There was never a plan in it.

Just work, and then more work, and then this. I'd do it the same way again.

9 There were months I paid to work Truck line 70s

People see these trucks and think this thing arrived like this. There was a stretch at the front of this where I wasn't making money. I mean actually not making money. Some months I was paying for the privilege of going to work.

Fuel, insurance, the payment on equipment, whatever broke that week. Add it up and there were months the business took more out of my pocket than it put in. And you still get up. You still run the route, because forty something families are waiting on you and they don't care about your math.

That went on longer than I'd like to admit. Months of it before we finally caught up and started turning an actual profit. Why say that part out loud? Because everybody posts the trucks.

Nobody posts the year you were underwater. And somebody out there is in their version of that right now. It got better. It got better because we kept showing up and people kept telling their neighbors.

That's the only strategy I've ever had.

10 I called to complain and signed three more years Truck line 80s

This actually happened to me, before any of this existed. I had a dumpster at a business. And I noticed they were charging me fifty dollars a month in fuel that wasn't there when I signed up. So I called to complain about it.

And here's the thing, they were nice about it. Really nice. Happy to come off the price, no argument, problem solved. I hung up feeling like I'd handled it.

What I'd actually done was sign a brand new three year contract to get that discount. That was the trade and I didn't catch it. The call where they help you is the call that costs you. That's the playbook.

You complain, they give you something small, and the price of that small thing is another three years of you. How many people do you figure have hung up that same phone call feeling good about it? 11 Seventy two dollars to a hundred twenty seven Truck line 78s

I remember this one distinctly because it's the reason I pay attention to it now. I signed up for a dumpster. A yard and a half, nothing fancy. First month's bill was seventy two dollars.

Three months later I was at a hundred twenty seven. Same dumpster. Same pickups. Same trash going in it.

Seventy six percent more money in one quarter and not one phone call telling me why. How does a price move that far that fast with nobody saying anything? It moves because it's built to move. The number you sign up at is a doorway.

Then the fuel line shows up. Then the environmental line, then the annual adjustment, and every one of them is small enough that you don't pick up the phone about it. Four small things is how you get from seventy two to a hundred twenty seven. Nobody ever has to lie to you.

Go look at what you paid your first month and what you paid last month. Pull both bills up right now, it takes a minute. If those two numbers are different and nobody told you why, you already know what I'm talking about.

13 I picked the people over the rates Truck line 59s

I didn't end up out here because I've never been anywhere else. Back in the curbing days I worked all over. San Marcos. Dallas.

San Antonio. Bigger markets, more houses per square mile, more money sitting on the table than there is out here in the Concho Valley. I've seen what those rates look like. And I came back here on purpose.

The people are just different. I know how that sounds coming from a guy who wants your business, but I'd say it either way. Somebody waves at a truck out here. Somebody hands you a bottle of water in August.

You skip a house by accident and they call you up like a neighbor instead of like a complaint. So would I make more somewhere else? Probably. I'd rather work for better people than better rates, and I've done enough of both to know the difference.
