01.1 The Protein that Decides How You Feel Deep

There is a protein most people have never heard of that can quietly change what every
hormone number on your page actually means. And when somebody tells you your labs are
normal but you feel awful, this is frequently the reason.

It is called sex hormone binding globulin. SHBG for short.

Its job is to bind to hormones and carry them through your bloodstream. Think of it as
transport. Hormone that is attached to it is in transit, not available to your tissues in
that moment. Albumin binds some as well, more weakly.

So picture two women with the exact same total testosterone. One has low SHBG, so most of
her testosterone is unbound and usable. The other has high SHBG, so most of hers is locked
up in transport. Same number on the page. Very different amount actually reaching her
tissues. Very different way of feeling.

That is the mechanism behind a normal looking result sitting right next to a genuinely not
normal set of symptoms. It is not a mystery, and it is not you exaggerating. It is
measurable.

Now, what moves SHBG. Several things, and they matter because most of them are things
people are already dealing with.

Thyroid function moves it. Liver function moves it. Insulin and your broader metabolic
health move it. And certain medications move it, including hormonal birth control, which
tends to raise it. So a woman on continuous birth control can be suppressing her own
production and raising the protein that binds up what is left, at the same time, from the
same medication.

Here is why I am telling you about a protein you will never think about again.

Free testosterone, the portion actually available to you, usually is not measured directly
in standard testing. It gets calculated. And the calculation needs the total, plus SHBG,
plus albumin. If nobody ordered SHBG, that calculation cannot be done at all. Which means
you may have been handed a total number and told it was fine, when the number that
actually correlates with how you feel was never produced.

That is not somebody being careless. Standard panels are built for standard questions.
This is a more specific question than the standard panel was designed to answer.

So what do you do with it.

You do not need to memorize any of this. You need one thing. When somebody tells you your
testosterone is normal, it is completely reasonable to ask whether SHBG was run and
whether a free level was calculated. If the answer is no, then what you have is one number
without the context that makes it mean anything.

A total by itself, with nothing next to it, can be genuinely misleading. And there is a
real, measurable explanation for why. That makes it a question worth asking rather than a
mystery you are supposed to live with.

means. Sometimes the more useful approach is treating what you are actually experiencing

produce a nearly identical symptom picture. Fatigue, weight changes, mood changes, brain
fog. If you treat hormones and miss a thyroid problem, you will spend months wondering why
the treatment underdelivered.

Then a lipid panel. Cholesterol and triglycerides. Partly because hormones and cholesterol
are connected, and partly because this is a reasonable moment to look. If something is out

cardiovascular markers.

If you are losing iron monthly, your stored iron can drift down while your blood count
still looks fine, and low iron produces the exact fatigue and fog people assume is
hormonal.

A number without your story is just data. Your story without numbers is guesswork. The
point of the draw is to put the two together.

The question I hear most is some version of this. If I start, am I locked in for life? And
I want to take it seriously, because that fear alone keeps a lot of women from ever asking

The short answer is no. It is not a single decision made once and locked in forever. It
gets revisited based on how you are actually doing, what your labs show, and what your

But I would rather give you the mechanism than just reassurance, because reassurance
without a reason underneath it is not worth much.

Here is what people are actually afraid of. They have heard that once you supply a hormone
from outside, your body stops making its own, and that if you stop you will crash below
where you started. So the fear is not really about commitment. It is about doing permanent
damage.

Let me separate those two.

The first part has truth in it. When you supply a hormone from outside the body, your own
production of it can decrease. That is not a malfunction. Your body runs on feedback
loops. It is constantly measuring what is present and adjusting what it produces
accordingly. If there is enough already, it makes less. That is the system working
correctly.

The second part, the permanent damage part, is where the fear outruns the biology. A
system responding to its inputs is a different thing from a system that has been broken.
When the input changes, the system responds again.

What is genuinely true, and what I want you to actually hear, is that stopping is
something you plan rather than something you do abruptly on a Tuesday because you ran out
or got frustrated. That is the real practical consideration, and it is a reason to stay in
contact with somebody rather than a reason to never start.

So what should you actually weigh.

Not "is this forever," because that is not the real question. The real questions are what
gets monitored, how often, what the plan is if you want to stop, and what the plan is if
something changes in your health. Those have concrete answers, and a clinician who knows
your history should be able to give them to you.

And I would add one thing that gets lost in this conversation. Deciding not to start is
also a decision with consequences. It gets treated as the neutral option, the safe
default, and it is not neutral. It is a different set of tradeoffs, and it deserves the
same scrutiny you are giving the other side.

Asking the question commits you to nothing. It just gets you real answers instead of
secondhand ones.

Here are the ten questions we get most about menopause and weight loss.

What is the actual difference between perimenopause and menopause?

Menopause is a single point in time. Twelve consecutive months without a period.
Everything before that point is perimenopause, and everything after it is post
menopause. The confusing part is that perimenopause is where most of the symptoms
actually live, and it can run for years. So women say I am not in menopause yet and treat
that as a reason not to look into any of this, when the transition is exactly the part
they are standing in.

Do I still need birth control during perimenopause?

Yes, until you have actually reached menopause. Fertility drops, but irregular cycles are
not the same thing as no ovulation. This catches people out, because the assumption is
that if your cycle has gone haywire the door must be closed. It is not, and that is worth
a specific conversation rather than an assumption.

Why am I gaining weight around my middle when my eating has not changed?

Two things are happening at once and they compound. Hormonal changes through the
transition shift where your body stores fat, toward the middle. And separately, muscle
mass declines with age unless you are actively working against it, which lowers what you
burn at rest. So the same intake lands differently than it used to. It is not a willpower
problem and it is not imaginary, but it does mean the strategy that worked at thirty will
not carry you through this on its own.

Where does the fat actually go when you lose weight?

You breathe most of it out. That is not a joke. When fat gets broken down it becomes
carbon dioxide and water. The carbon dioxide dissolves into your blood, travels to your
lungs, and you exhale it. Roughly eighty four percent of the mass you lose leaves as
carbon dioxide, and the other sixteen percent leaves as water, through urine, sweat, and
vapor. So if you lose ten pounds of fat, about eight and a half pounds of it left through
your lungs. It is not magic, it is biochemistry, but it does reframe what burning fat
actually means.

If I am on a GLP-1 for weight loss, am I losing muscle along with the fat?

Rapid weight loss of any kind can cost you lean mass, and that is a real concern rather
than a talking point, because muscle is what protects your metabolism and your bone.
Which is why resistance training and adequate protein are not optional add ons here.
They are part of the treatment. We talk about resistance training with essentially every
person on one of these medications. The honest difficulty is not knowing that. It is
getting it actually implemented.

What happens to my weight if I stop taking a GLP-1?

Appetite generally returns, and for a lot of people weight follows unless something else
has changed in the meantime. That is not the medication failing. That is the medication
no longer being present. Which is exactly why the habits built during that window matter
so much. The medication creates a period where changes are easier to make. What you do
with that period determines what happens after it.

Can hormone therapy help me lose weight?

I would not frame it that way, and I would be careful with anyone who does. What
addressing hormones can do is make some of the other work possible. Better sleep, better
energy, better recovery, a better ability to hold onto muscle. Those things support
weight management. It is not a weight loss treatment, and promising it as one sets people
up to feel like it failed them.

My weight loss stalled and I did not change anything. Why?

Because you did change something, just not on purpose. As you lose weight you get
smaller, and a smaller body burns less. Meanwhile if lean mass came off along with the
fat, that drops further still. So the plan that produced the first stretch of results is
mathematically no longer the same plan. A stall is usually a signal to adjust the inputs,
particularly protein and resistance training, rather than evidence that something is
broken.

Are hot flashes and night sweats the only symptoms of menopause?

Not close. Those are the two that made it into the cultural script, so those are the ones
women feel entitled to mention. Sleep disruption, mood changes, brain fog, joint aches,
changes in libido, and genitourinary symptoms like dryness, discomfort, and recurrent
urinary tract infections are all part of this picture too. That last group in particular
