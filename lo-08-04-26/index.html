01 Why Your Labs Can Look Normal While You Feel Terrible Long form

This video explains why perimenopause and menopause can cause very real symptoms even when a hormone lab looks normal, and where testosterone and hormone therapy actually fit into that picture. It's for any woman who has been told nothing is wrong but does not feel like herself.

WHAT PERIMENOPAUSE ACTUALLY IS

If you're in your late thirties or forties and it feels like your body quietly changed the rules on you, you're probably not imagining it. Perimenopause is not a single moment. It's a transition, and for a lot of women it starts years before a period ever becomes noticeably irregular.

Most people think perimenopause means hot flashes and skipped cycles right before menopause. In real life, it often shows up first as things that don't look hormonal at all. Sleep that used to come easily suddenly doesn't. A mood that feels less steady. A brain that feels less sharp. Those changes can start well before anyone thinks to connect them to hormones, and by the time someone puts the pieces together, they've often been living with it for a while.

I'm Tiffany Nunez, a nurse practitioner here at Live Oak. I'm also a Menopause Society Certified Practitioner, which is a specific credential through The Menopause Society. In plain terms, it means I've completed additional focused training in perimenopause and menopause care, beyond general practice. I bring that up because this topic deserves more than a surface level answer, and I want you to know where this information is coming from.

WHY ONE LAB TEST CAN MISLEAD

One of the most common things I hear is, I got my hormones tested and everything came back normal, so I was told nothing is wrong. I understand why that's confusing, and I want to explain why it happens.

In perimenopause, hormone levels don't decline in a smooth, predictable line. They can shift week to week, sometimes even within the same cycle, rising and falling in ways a single number can't capture. A single blood draw only captures one moment in a pattern that's constantly moving. It's a snapshot, not the whole picture.

That doesn't mean labs are useless. They're one piece of information, and they matter. But a normal result on one particular day doesn't automatically rule out a hormonal shift. It often just means the test caught you on a steadier day. That's not a flaw in the test. It's more a limitation of testing something that's naturally in motion.

This is part of why perimenopause can be tricky to catch from bloodwork alone, and why timing and context matter as much as the result itself. Your symptoms and your history matter just as much as the numbers on a lab report, sometimes more.

THE SYMPTOM LIST NO ONE CONNECTS

Here's where it gets confusing for a lot of women. The symptoms of perimenopause rarely show up as one obvious thing. They tend to show up as a cluster, and each piece on its own can look like something else entirely.

Trouble falling asleep or staying asleep. A mood that feels off, more irritable or more flat than usual. Brain fog, where words or names feel just out of reach. Joint aches that show up without an injury to explain them. A lower interest in sex. Weight that seems to redistribute toward the midsection even when nothing about your eating or activity has changed.

On their own, any one of those can be blamed on stress, on aging, on a bad month, or on being busy. Often they get addressed one at a time, without anyone stepping back to ask whether they're connected. That's often the piece that's missing. Looking at the whole pattern together, instead of one symptom in isolation, tends to be what actually points toward what's going on, and that's often the difference between feeling dismissed and actually getting somewhere.

NORMAL LABS VERSUS HOW YOU FEEL

I want to spend a minute on something that trips a lot of people up. Your labs are normal and you feel terrible are not contradictory statements. They're answering two different questions.

A lab value can tell us where a hormone level sits against a general reference range at one point in time. It doesn't tell us how your particular body responds to that level, and it doesn't capture how you're sleeping, how your mood has shifted, or how you feel in your own life day to day. Two people can have a similar lab result and feel completely different, and that gap between the number and the experience is exactly where a lot of women get stuck.

If you've been told your labs are fine and left it at that, even though something clearly feels different, that's a reasonable thing to bring back to a clinician and ask about further. Feeling unwell is information too. It deserves the same weight as a number on a page, and it's worth a longer conversation rather than letting a single test be the end of the discussion.

WHAT HORMONE THERAPY IS AND ISN'T

Let's talk plainly about what hormone therapy actually is. In general terms, it's a way of supplementing hormones like estrogen, progesterone, or testosterone when the body is producing less than it used to, with the goal of easing symptoms tied to that decline. That's the concept in its simplest form.

Here's what it is not. It's not a fountain of youth. It's not a guarantee that every symptom disappears. It's not one size fits all, and it's not something that gets started without a real look at your labs, your history, and your goals first. It's not simple, and it's not automatic. It deserves more thought than that.

It's also not an all or nothing, forever or never decision made in a single visit. It's a conversation that can evolve as your body changes and as more information comes in. The specifics, like what might be used and how a plan gets adjusted over time, belong between a patient and their clinician, based on that person's own labs and history. That's not me avoiding the question. That's genuinely how individualized this is.

HOW THE CONVERSATION ON RISK CHANGED

If you're hesitant about hormone therapy because of something you heard years ago, you're not alone, and your hesitation makes sense. The public conversation around hormone therapy and its risks has genuinely shifted over the last couple of decades. Understanding in this area has evolved as more research and more clinical experience have come in.

I'm not going to throw numbers or specific studies at you here, partly because that's not useful without your specific context, and partly because risk isn't a single fixed number that applies the same way to every person. Risk depends on things like your age, your personal and family health history, other conditions you may have, and the specific approach being considered. What's reasonable for one person may not be reasonable for another, and that's true in both directions.

What I can tell you is that this is an active, evolving area of medicine, and it deserves a real conversation rather than a decision based on something you heard secondhand years ago. I'm also not going to sit here and tell you hormone therapy is safe for you specifically. I don't know your history well enough to say that in a video, and no clinician should. That conversation belongs with a provider who actually knows your labs, your history, and your risk factors, not with a video on the internet.

TESTOSTERONE IS A WOMEN'S HORMONE TOO

Here's something that surprises a lot of women. Testosterone isn't just a male hormone. It's present in the female body too, and it plays a role in energy, mood, muscle tone, and sex drive. Levels are naturally lower than in men, but it's still an active part of a woman's hormone picture, and it can decline as part of the same perimenopause and menopause transition we've been talking about.

Because testosterone gets talked about almost exclusively in the context of men, it's often left out of the conversation entirely when women bring up low energy or low libido. Estrogen and progesterone tend to get most of the attention, and testosterone can get overlooked, even though for some women it's a meaningful piece of the picture. That gap in awareness isn't anyone's fault. It's just a conversation that hasn't caught up yet.

I'm not saying every woman with low energy has a testosterone issue. That would be an oversimplification, and this is exactly the kind of thing that needs an actual conversation and actual labs rather than a guess. But if no one has ever mentioned testosterone to you as a woman, that's worth knowing. It's a legitimate part of this conversation, not a footnote.

IS THIS CONVERSATION FOR YOU

So who tends to be a reasonable candidate for this conversation? I want to be careful here, because there isn't a checklist that applies to everyone, and that's the honest answer, not a dodge. There's no single lab number or single symptom that decides it on its own.

In general, it's worth having this conversation if you're noticing a pattern of changes rather than one isolated symptom, especially around sleep, mood, energy, weight, or libido, and it's been going on for a while rather than a rough week or two. It's also worth having if you've already been told your labs are normal but you don't feel like yourself, or if you're simply curious what your options actually are before deciding anything.

It's just as reasonable to have this conversation and land on watching and waiting, or trying other changes first, as it is to explore hormone therapy. Bringing questions to a clinician doesn't commit you to anything. And if you do move forward, it's not an all or nothing choice made once and locked in forever. It's something that gets reassessed over time, based on how you're actually doing.

If any part of what we've covered today sounded like your own experience, that's reason enough to ask, not reason enough to assume you already know the answer.

WHERE TO GO FROM HERE

Let's bring this together. Perimenopause often starts earlier than people expect, and it rarely announces itself clearly. A single lab test is a snapshot, not the whole story, and normal labs and feeling unwell can both be true at the same time without contradicting each other. The symptoms tend to cluster together instead of showing up as one obvious sign, which is part of why they get missed or blamed on something else.

Hormone therapy is a real, individualized option, not something that works the same way for everyone, and testosterone is part of that picture for women too, even though it rarely gets mentioned. The conversation around risk has genuinely evolved, and what's reasonable depends on you, your history, and your own clinician's judgment, not on a headline from years ago or a video on the internet.

If any of this sounded familiar, you don't have to figure it out alone or piece it together from what you read online. A conversation with a provider who actually knows your labs and your history is a reasonable next step, whenever you're ready for it.

02 Why Low Desire Isn't Only a Relationship Problem Long form
9 sectionsabout 13 min

Want changes? Hit Copy script, paste it wherever you like to work, edit it however you want, and email it back to landon@cosmicarcmedia.com. It goes into the teleprompter exactly the way you send it.

Myda Fay, nurse practitioner at Live Oak, walks through why low sexual desire in women is often physiological rather than purely personal or relational. This is for any woman who has noticed her desire change and assumed it was just stress, her relationship, or getting older.

LOW DESIRE GETS MISREAD

A lot of women come in and start the conversation with an apology. They'll say something like, I don't know what's wrong with me, or things have just changed and I don't know why. Almost never do they start with, I think this might be physical.

That's not their fault. When desire drops off, the first explanations people reach for are usually about character or about the relationship. Am I just not that interested anymore. Is something wrong between us. Is this just what happens after years together, or after kids, or after forty.

Those are reasonable questions to ask. But they're not the only questions, and they're often not the right first ones. Desire is regulated by hormones, by sleep, by stress, by physical comfort, and by mental health, in addition to whatever is happening in a relationship. When one or more of those systems shifts, desire can shift with it, independent of how a person feels about their partner or their life.

I want to be clear that this isn't about replacing the relationship conversation. It's about not skipping the physiological one. If a woman has been with the same partner for twenty years and desire drops off over a period of months, the relationship might be part of the story. It might not be the whole story. Treating low desire as automatically a referendum on the relationship can send someone looking in the wrong place for a long time.

DESIRE IS NOT AROUSAL

Most people use desire and arousal as if they're the same word. Clinically, they're not, and the difference actually matters for figuring out what's going on.

Desire is the wanting. It's the mental interest, the pull toward intimacy before anything physical has happened. Arousal is the body's response once something is already underway. You can have one without the other, and that surprises people the first time they hear it.

Some women have plenty of physical response once they're in the moment, but they rarely feel the pull to get there in the first place. That's a desire issue. Other women want to be intimate, think about it, look forward to it, but the body doesn't respond the way it used to once things start. That's more of an arousal issue.

They can also show up together, and they can share some of the same root causes, but not in every case. A hormonal shift might affect one more than the other. Stress might affect one more than the other. This is part of why a generic answer rarely fits everybody, and why it helps to actually name which one a person is experiencing before assuming a cause. Getting specific about which one you're noticing is often the most useful piece of information you can bring into that conversation.

THE HORMONAL CONTRIBUTORS

Hormones are a genuine piece of this picture, and there's more than one of them worth knowing about.

Estrogen plays a role in blood flow and tissue comfort, and when it drops, intimacy can become less comfortable, which on its own can lower interest over time. Progesterone shifts alongside estrogen through different life stages and can affect mood and sleep, both of which touch desire indirectly.

Testosterone gets talked about mostly in men, but women produce it too, in smaller amounts, and it can play a role in desire for many women. When it's low, some women notice a real drop in interest that isn't explained by anything else going on in their life.

Thyroid function belongs in this conversation as well. An underactive thyroid can bring fatigue, low mood, and a general sense of running on empty, and desire tends to be one of the first things to fade when someone feels that depleted.

None of these hormones work in isolation, and normal ranges vary quite a bit from person to person. Two women can have similar lab numbers and feel completely different day to day. That's exactly why this tends to require an actual conversation and actual labs rather than a guess based on age or a symptom checklist alone.

WHAT ELSE CAN BE GOING ON

Hormones are one piece, but they're not the whole picture, and I don't want to give the impression that every case of low desire traces back to a lab value.

Sleep is a big one. Chronic poor sleep affects hormone regulation, mood, and energy all at once, and desire tends to sit near the bottom of the list when someone is running on empty. Stress works the same way. A body that's managing a heavy stress load for months or years tends to deprioritize desire, because from a physiological standpoint, desire is not an urgent function.

Certain medications can lower desire as a side effect, including some common ones for mood, blood pressure, and other everyday conditions. That's worth knowing, not as a reason to stop anything on your own, but as a reason to mention it during an evaluation.

Pelvic floor issues and physical discomfort during intimacy matter too. If intimacy has become uncomfortable or painful, it makes complete sense that interest would drop as a kind of protective response. That's not something to push through quietly. It's something to bring up.

And mental health belongs in this list plainly. Depression and anxiety can both affect desire, and treating one can genuinely help the other, which is one more reason this deserves a real conversation instead of a single explanation.

WHY THIS RARELY COMES UP

Here's something I notice constantly. Women mention a headache, a rash, trouble sleeping, all without hesitation. Low desire is different. It tends to sit quietly for months or years before anyone says it out loud in an appointment.

Part of that is embarrassment, even when there's nothing to be embarrassed about. Part of it is the assumption that it's not really a medical topic, that it belongs in a different conversation entirely, maybe with a partner, maybe with no one. And part of it is simply not knowing that anyone can actually help.

A well woman visit is a natural place to bring this up, precisely because it's already a visit built around the whole picture, not just one complaint. It's a space where hormones, mood, sleep, and physical comfort are already normal topics, not exceptions.

I'd encourage anyone who has been sitting on this to say it plainly at that visit. You don't need the perfect words. Something as simple as, I've noticed my desire has changed and I want to understand why, is a complete and reasonable way to start. That one sentence gives a provider enough to begin asking the right questions.

WHAT AN EVALUATION ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

I think it helps to know what actually happens once you raise this, because the unknown is part of what keeps people quiet.

It typically starts with a conversation, not a test. Questions about when the change started, whether it was gradual or sudden, what else has shifted around the same time, sleep, mood, stress, any new medications, and whether intimacy itself has become uncomfortable. That history usually points toward which systems are worth a closer look.

From there, bloodwork is common, looking at relevant hormone levels along with thyroid function and anything else the history suggests. Labs are one input, not the whole answer. They get read alongside how a person actually feels, not as a standalone verdict.

A physical exam may be part of it too, particularly if discomfort during intimacy is part of the picture, since that can point toward a physical cause that's separate from desire itself.

The goal of all of this is to build a fuller picture of what's actually contributing, rather than assuming one cause because it's the most common one. Two women with the same complaint can walk out of that evaluation with two different explanations, because two different sets of contributing factors were actually at play.

THE RANGE OF APPROACHES

Once a picture starts to form, there's a range of ways to approach this, and I want to walk through the categories generally rather than any specific plan, because the right approach depends entirely on what's actually driving things for that person.

If a hormonal contributor is identified, hormone level optimization may be part of the conversation, tailored to that person's labs and history. If sleep, stress, or mental health are playing a significant role, addressing those directly can meaningfully change desire on its own, sometimes more than people expect. If physical discomfort during intimacy is part of the picture, that gets addressed as its own thread, because comfort tends to be a prerequisite for desire, not a side note.

There are also procedural options some people ask about, and whether any of those make sense is an individual conversation, not something I'd generalize here.

What I want you to take from this section isn't a specific plan. It's that there isn't one universal answer that applies to everyone, and that's actually good news. It means an approach can be built around what's really going on for you, instead of a one size fits all answer that may not touch the actual cause.

THIS IS COMMON AND WORTH RAISING

If there's one thing I want to land clearly, it's this. Changes in desire are common. They show up across different life stages, different hormone patterns, different stress loads, and different relationships. You are not the only person sitting with this question, even though it can feel isolating.

Common doesn't mean nothing can be done about it, and it doesn't mean you have to just live with it either. In many cases, once the actual contributing factors are identified, whether that's hormonal, physical, or related to sleep, stress, or mood, there are ways to address them. Not every case resolves the same way, and I'm not going to promise a specific outcome here, because that depends on what's actually driving it for you. But treatable in many cases is a fair and honest way to describe this.

The bigger point is that this deserves to be treated like any other health concern. Not as something to quietly absorb, not as evidence that something is wrong with you as a person, and not as a problem you have to solve alone before you're allowed to ask for help. It's a legitimate topic for a medical visit, the same as your blood pressure or your sleep.

So, quick recap. Low desire is often physiological before it's anything else, even though it tends to get filed under relationship problems or personal failing. Desire and arousal are related but different, and knowing which one you're noticing helps narrow down what's actually happening. Hormones like estrogen, progesterone, testosterone, and thyroid function can all play a role, alongside sleep, stress, medications, physical comfort, and mental health.

A lab result inside the normal range can still reflect a system that's working harder than it should to get that result.

A lab result can sit inside the normal range and still tell an incomplete story. Here's what I mean.

Insulin's job is to help move sugar out of your bloodstream and into your cells. When cells start responding to insulin less easily than they used to, your body compensates by producing more of it to get the same result.

For a while, that compensation can work well enough that a basic number, like fasting blood sugar, still looks normal on paper. But normal, in that case, doesn't mean effortless. It can mean your body is working harder behind the scenes to hold a result that used to come easily.

That gap between a normal looking number and how much effort it took to get there is often invisible on a standard test. It's also part of why someone can be told their labs are fine and still feel like something is off. Normal and optimal are not always the same thing, and that gap is worth looking at individually rather than assuming one number tells the whole story.

A first conversation about the causes behind fatigue looks like a broader, individualized approach, not one special test or a guaranteed outcome.

If you're picturing a root cause conversation as one special test that unlocks the whole answer, here's what to actually expect instead.

It starts with a real history. Sleep patterns, stress, energy through the day, family history, and how long fatigue has been building, not just whether it's present right now. That context matters as much as any single number.

From there, it usually means looking at metabolic and hormone function together, rather than as two separate appointments that never compare notes with each other. A normal result on one piece doesn't end the conversation. It becomes one data point inside a larger pattern.

What you shouldn't expect is a guarantee, a fixed timeline, or the same plan someone else got. What's relevant for your fatigue, whether that leans more metabolic, more hormonal, more about sleep, or some combination, is going to look different from the next person's.

So expect a conversation that takes your whole picture seriously, and expect it to be specific to you rather than a standard checklist applied the same way to everyone.
