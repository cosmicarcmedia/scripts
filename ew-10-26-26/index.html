The scripts

1 The fire station you can sleep in Old Central Fire Station 82s

Most people drive past this building downtown and assume it's still a working fire station. It hasn't put out a fire since 1976. This is the old Central Fire Station, built in 1928. The architects were Trost and Trost out of El Paso, the firm behind a good chunk of that city's skyline.

It cost thirty-two thousand dollars. So what's the tower for? Canvas fire hose comes back from a call soaking wet, and it has to hang straight down to dry or it rots. That four story tower was the hose dryer.

After the trucks moved out in 1976, it spent about twenty years as a city recreation and senior center. Then a nonprofit used it for a while. Then in 2017 a local couple bought it. Today it's a bed and breakfast with a pizzeria and a taproom downstairs.

You can book a suite upstairs and spend the night in a 1928 fire station. The walls are covered in helmets, jackets, and old photos that people around town donated. As a firefighter, I'll admit this one gets me. It did its job for almost fifty years and then found a second one.

Next time you drive down Magdalen, look up at the tower.

2 Read the back of your smoke alarm Old Central Fire Station 74s

I'm a firefighter, and when I walk through a house somebody's about to buy, I look up before I look at the countertops. I'm checking the smoke alarms. Most people don't know smoke alarms expire. The recommendation is to replace them every ten years.

And the date it was made is printed right on the back. So in a house built twenty years ago, how old are the alarms on the ceiling? A lot of the time, nobody knows. And a chirping alarm usually gets its battery pulled and never gets it back.

The National Fire Protection Association keeps the guidance simple. One inside every bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, and one on every level of the house. Test them once a month. Close to three out of five home fire deaths happen in homes with no smoke alarms or alarms that didn't work.

That's a cheap problem to fix. A new alarm costs less than dinner out. So when you get the keys, pull one down and read the date. If it's past ten years, swap them all before you unpack a single box.

3 Same house, different insurance bill Old Central Fire Station 74s

Two houses can be the same size, the same age, and the same price, and one can cost a lot more to insure. A big piece of that comes down to the fire department. Every community gets a public protection class from a company called ISO. It runs from one to ten, and one is the best.

It's based on things like the fire department, the water supply, and how 911 calls get handled. Insurance companies use that number when they price a homeowners policy. So why would a house a few miles out cost more? Distance.

If a home sits more than about five road miles from a responding fire station, it can land in the worst class. Being far from a hydrant can hurt it too. And as a firefighter, I get why. Every minute on the road is a minute the fire keeps growing.

I'm not telling you to skip the country house. I love the country house. Just get an insurance quote before you fall in love with it. That way the monthly payment you planned on is the one you get.

4 The Christmas tree rule firefighters repeat Old Central Fire Station 71s

Every December, firefighters all over the country end up repeating the same few things about Christmas trees. I'm one of them, so you're getting my version. A real tree looks great on day one. By week three, if nobody's been watering it, it's kindling standing in your living room.

And a dry tree burns fast. So how do you keep yours from getting there? Start at the lot. Pick one with needles that don't fall off when you touch them.

Cut a couple of inches off the bottom of the trunk before it goes in the stand. Then add water every single day. It drinks more than you'd think. Keep it at least three feet from anything that puts out heat, like the fireplace, a space heater, a vent, or candles.

Turn the lights off when you go to bed or leave the house. And when the needles start dropping after Christmas, it's time for it to go. None of that takes the fun out of it. It just means you decide when the tree comes down.

5 Closed in 1983 and still boarded up Concho Avenue 60s

This building went dark in 1983, the same year the Texas Theatre closed. It's the old Naylor Hotel, and it's been sitting here boarded up ever since. It's had more names than most people have had addresses. The Nimitz, the Landon, the Naylor, and the Town House.

And it's burned more than once. A fire in 1902 killed a woman named Rosa Landon, and it burned again in 1925. So what you're looking at is the rebuild. The six story hotel went up in the 1930s.

And why did it close? It couldn't pass fire code. As a firefighter, that part makes sense to me. Downtown's been filling back in around it, one building at a time.

Lofts, restaurants, people living down here again. So what happens when somebody finally takes this one on? Whoever does gets one of the best corners in town. Keep an eye on it.

6 A bordello above a jewelry store Concho Avenue 59s

Most people walk down Concho Avenue and look in the shop windows. Look one floor higher. For about half a century, the second floor of this building was a bordello. Miss Hattie's.

It ran right over the main street downtown. How long can a place like that last on the main street in town? Long enough that it took the Texas Rangers to finally close it. Decades later, in the late 1970s, the building changed hands and the upstairs got remodeled.

Today it's Miss Hattie's Bordello Museum. The rooms are set up with furnishings from that era. And you get to it through the jewelry store downstairs. That's my favorite part.

You walk in to look at rings and walk out knowing one of the wildest chapters in San Angelo history. Tours can't be booked ahead, so just stop in. And next time you're on Concho, look up.

7 A pink pearl from the Concho River Concho Avenue 52s

There's a pearl on this street about the size of a pea, and it's priced at a hundred ten thousand dollars. It came out of a river a few blocks from here. Concho pearls come from a freshwater mussel that lives in these rivers. They don't come out white.

They come out pink, purple, and lavender, and usually a little lopsided. So why would anybody pay that much for one? Because a round one is rare. The one at Legend Jewelers is just over twelve millimeters and close to perfectly round.

Legend's been on this street since 1976. Ask to see the case next time you're downtown. And here's what I like about it. A lot of towns have a river.

Not many have one that makes its own gemstones.

8 Guess the oldest building downtown Concho Avenue 59s

Quick quiz, and be honest, pick before I give you the answer. Which of these three downtown buildings is the oldest? The old Central Fire Station with the hose tower on Magdalen. The Orient railroad depot on Chadbourne.

Or a two story sandstone building right here on Concho Avenue. Got your pick? The depot feels like the safe answer, because railroads feel old. The depot went up in 1909 and 1910.

The fire station came in 1928. And the sandstone building on Concho beats them both by more than twenty years. It's the Freeze Building, built in 1887 as a blacksmith shop. A Danish immigrant named Peter Freeze put it up after a fire wiped out his first shop that spring.

It got restored in the 1990s and went on the National Register in 1997. How'd you do? Tell me in the comments which one you picked.

9 The railroad that was headed for the Pacific Orient depot 62s

A man named Arthur Stilwell planned a railroad sixteen hundred miles long, from Kansas City to the Pacific coast of Mexico. San Angelo was on the route. It was called the Kansas City, Mexico and Orient. This depot went up in 1909 and 1910, and it was the biggest one on the whole line.

It was the railroad's Texas headquarters. So did it ever reach the ocean? Never. The Mexican Revolution got in the way, and the full line never got finished.

Santa Fe bought it in 1928. Then in 1989, Santa Fe planned to tear this building down. People here wouldn't let that happen. They rehabbed it, and today it's the Railway Museum of San Angelo, with a senior center in the old depot buildings.

It's run by volunteers and open on Saturdays. The dream never got finished, but the building it left behind is still standing on Chadbourne. Go see it on a Saturday.

10 Why there are painted sheep everywhere Orient depot 56s

If you've lived here a while, you've probably stopped noticing the painted sheep. There are about a hundred of them around town. So why sheep? Because at one point San Angelo was the largest sheep market in the United States.

Sheep ranching came to this area in 1877. Ranchers, cattlemen, and the railroads turned this town into one of the biggest wool and mohair markets in the country. In 1906 the local paper reported a single sale of more than half a million pounds of wool. And a lot of it left town on rails like these.

The sheep you see downtown started in 2007. A plain fiberglass ewe runs about sixteen hundred dollars before anybody paints it. So every one of them is somebody's sixteen hundred dollar decision. How many have you seen?

Next time you're downtown, count them.

11 The fort the town grew up around Fort Concho 67s

San Angelo started as the little town across the river from this fort. Fort Concho went up in 1867, and the town grew up to serve it. From 1875 to 1882, this was the regimental headquarters of the 10th Cavalry, the Buffalo Soldiers. Their commander was Colonel Benjamin Grierson.

The army pulled out in 1889. So how is so much of it still standing? Because the city stepped in. San Angelo has owned and run it since 1935.

In 1961 it became a National Historic Landmark. The historic district covers forty acres of original buildings, reconstructions, and one stabilized ruin. Today it's a museum you can walk right into. And the best time to see it is coming up.

Christmas at Old Fort Concho runs December fourth through sixth this year. The whole fort fills up for it. If you've never been inside the fort, that's the weekend to go. And if you're house shopping, the streets around it carry some of the oldest history in town.

12 The county seat that washed away Fort Concho 64s

San Angelo wasn't the first county seat around here. The first one washed away in a single morning. It was a town called Ben Ficklin, laid out in 1873 on the South Concho. In 1875 it beat out San Angela for the county seat.

By 1879 about six hundred people lived there. Then came the night of August twenty-third, 1882. It rained hard all night, and by midmorning the next day the water hit town. Sixty-five people drowned.

On the flat, almost nothing was left. Only the courthouse, the jail, and two houses were still standing. So where do you put a county seat when the county seat is gone? You move it to the town across the river from this fort.

That's how San Angelo became the county seat in 1883. It's also why I tell buyers to pull the flood map on any lot near the water before they make an offer. These rivers have done it before.
