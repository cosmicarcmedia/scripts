The scripts

1
I sold liquor for twenty five years and bought books the whole time
The origin and the face
▾
Felton is a proprietor with a True West award and a Texas Country Reporter segment, and almost nobody in town knows he built the collection while working a liquor wholesaler's route. That detour is what makes a stranger trust him with a book recommendation.
Spot A
I sold liquor for twenty five years. And the whole time, I was buying books. It started at North Texas, in the early sixties. I was working at Voertman's bookstore in Denton, and I bought a copy of The Wild Bunch, the book about Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid. I've been buying Texas books ever since.
Spot B
In 1963 I took a job at a liquor store in Dallas. That turned into a career in sales with liquor wholesalers. Twenty five years of it. I kept collecting the whole time. Texana, Western Americana, anything about Texas history. It's such a wonderful, diverse history. Nearly every day, I still find something I didn't know about Texas. After a while I got tired of the rat race.
Spot C
So in 1995 I came home to San Angelo, brought my books with me, and opened Cactus Book Shop at 4 East Concho Avenue. The first book I sold in this shop was [OWNER: the title of the first book you sold in 1995]. In 2020, True West Magazine named us the Best Western History Book Store. They did it again every year through 2024. I'm quite proud of that. These days, people bring me their old books and I buy them. I've been at this long enough that some of them are books I sold their grandfather. I love getting up to come to work, and I love what I'm doing.
2
The house on Concho Avenue people ask me about
The local anchor
▾
Felton wrote this research up himself and customers ask him about it in the shop, so the video is already a conversation he has every week. It puts his street and his habit of checking the city directories on camera, and it gives the town a reason to share it.
Spot B
People come into my shop on Concho Avenue and ask if I have a book about Miss Hattie's. I don't. What I have is the research I did myself. I went through the old San Angelo city directories, and I checked parts of it with the West Texas Collection at Angelo State. The real house stood two blocks down, at 110 West Concho. It was a bordello, and it belonged to a woman named Cynthia Malone.
Spot A
She came to San Angelo in 1909 and ran that house for decades. For years, the city directories list her under another name, Grace Hallaway. Later they list her as Cynthia Malone. She built up an estate, ranch land and commercial property in and around town. She's buried at Fairmount Cemetery, and carved under her real name on the stone is her nickname. Honey.
Spot C
Her two story house is still standing. Somebody bought it in 1956 and moved it to 602 Elm Street, across the freeway from Central High School. You can drive by and look at it. I worked at the Roxy Theater on this same street back in 1953, so I've been looking at Concho Avenue for a long time. I wrote the whole thing up on my website, under The Real Bordello of Concho Avenue. Or come by the shop and ask me. I'm here Monday through Saturday, ten to five.
3
I don't buy my books on the internet
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
A bookshop owner who says out loud how his books arrive, what goes into a listing, and how his orders work gives a collector a reason to call him that a big online seller cannot match. The Kelton prices, the Lee McElroy detail and the order terms come straight from his own web pages.
Spot C
I don't buy my books on the internet. People bring them to me. They walk in that door with a stack, and I look through it. I'm proud to say I've got a reputation as a fair buyer. So when a book lands on my shelf, I've held it in my hands.
Spot A
That's why I can tell you what's in the one I'm holding. Hardback with the dust jacket, or without it. First edition, or a later printing. Signed or unsigned. One of my county histories still has the errata sheet laid in. That goes in the listing, because somebody out there is looking for exactly that copy. And if you order from me and don't like the book, send it back within seven days for a full refund. Any reason.
Spot B
Take Elmer Kelton. I have a Kelton paperback for about twenty two dollars, a TCU Press reprint of The Time It Never Rained. I also have a first edition of The Man Who Rode Midnight, 1986, Doubleday, for a hundred and fifty. Same writer, and a very different thing to hold. Elmer Kelton wrote one book under another name, Lee McElroy. Eyes of the Hawk, 1975, first edition, Doubleday. I list it that way, so you know exactly what you're buying. If you want something I haven't listed, call me. I might have it.
4
How to find your family in a Texas county history
The useful one
▾
Genealogists already walk into this shop for the county histories, and this gives a stranger a first step they can use today. Every detail comes from how Felton lists the books on his own site.
Spot A
If your family has been in Texas a while, there's a good chance they're in a book. It's a county history, the kind a county historical commission puts together. I've got over seventy linear feet of them on my shelves. Genealogists come in for them.
Spot B
Start at the index and look up your family name. A lot of these books have a section of family histories, set up in alphabetical order. Then look at what else is in the back. The Hardeman County book has the 1880 census rolls, marriage and birth records, and cattle brands. The Brewster County book lists the county's registered brands, and it has sketches of the early residents. The Mason County book has a cemetery map. Ask me about a particular county and I'll tell you what's in the back of it.
Spot C
One more thing. Check how many volumes there are. The McCulloch County handbook runs three. Volume two carries corrections and a supplemental index for volume one. So if you only buy volume one, you're missing the fixes. A lot of these books are out of print, and I have many more than I list. Tell me your county and what family you're chasing. I might have it.
One more idea
Take them home and mail me a check

Tell the story of a time you let a customer take books home on trust, and say why you do it. Open on the moment you handed her the stack, then show the shelf the books came off. Susan W.'s letter in Texas Co-op Power gives you the line to build around.
