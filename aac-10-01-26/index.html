2
What the City of Abilene wants from your alarm
The local anchor
▾
Hyperlocal and free to give away: the permit and false alarm schedule comes straight from the City of Abilene's own documents, and it makes you the company that knows the city's rules.
Spot B
If you have an alarm system in Abilene, the city is counting your false alarms. You get three a year for free. The fourth and fifth cost fifty dollars each. The sixth and seventh cost seventy-five. From the eighth on, it's a hundred each. Reach eight in twelve months and the city can pull your permit.
Spot C
You also need a permit just to have the system. It's twenty dollars a year for a home and fifty for a business. It's good for a year, and it expires on the last day of the month you got it. Run an alarm on an expired permit and that's a misdemeanor, up to five hundred dollars. The application asks for two people who've agreed to come to your house if the police call, let them in, and reset the system. Pick people who answer their phones.
Spot A
So what counts as a false alarm? Any police response when nothing was wrong. A malfunction counts too, which means a dying battery can cost you. On our Platinum plan, we replace those for free. A storm that sets it off doesn't count against you. The permit belongs to whoever holds it, and it doesn't come with the house. So if you buy a place that already has an alarm, you need your own. You can apply and pay online through the City of Abilene's alarm permit site.
3
Ask your alarm company for this number
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
This is the one that gets forwarded: the minute and a half of math comes from your own home page, and it hands viewers one question to put to any alarm company.
Spot C
A minute and a half. That's how long some alarm companies can take before anybody acts on your alarm. Forty-five seconds for the signal to reach their station, and forty-five more for the station to respond. That's a long time when somebody's already inside. A good monitoring station averages thirty to thirty-five seconds to respond. Our target is twenty or less, and our communicators send within seconds.
Spot A
Our average over the last twelve months is [OWNER: current average, the site says 12.6 seconds]. Does yours even have a number? When our signal lands, the operator can see which door tripped, front, back, or a window. If it's a fire, they may be able to tell the fire department which part of the house it's in.
Spot B
Six monitoring stations share the calls, so a storm at one of them doesn't leave you waiting. The station that watches AAC systems won Central Station of the Year in 2014, ahead of more than a hundred and eighty others. Call us with a question and a real person answers. Some companies send you to an answering service in another country. So ask your alarm company for their average response time. Then ask what a service call costs. On our Platinum plan, it's free during business hours, and a part that malfunctions gets replaced at no charge.
4
How burglars try to beat an alarm
The useful one
▾
It costs you nothing to give away and it earns the follow: the crash and smash and phone line explanation is already on your residential page, and it ends on two questions a homeowner can ask any installer.
Spot A
Kick in the door. Smash the panel. The alarm never calls anybody. Burglars have tried exactly that. Your panel gives you thirty to sixty seconds to type your code, and that's the window they use. So what happens if the panel waits for that countdown to finish before it calls the station? Smash it first, and the whole system goes quiet.
Spot B
Ours doesn't wait. The moment the entry door opens, the system sends a signal. It says a door just opened, stand by for a disarm confirmation. Type your code and the confirmation goes through, so nothing happens. Don't, and the station assumes the panel got smashed. They follow the normal burglary protocol and send police if they need to. The panel is tamper-resistant, too, and it's designed to keep working through attempted damage.
Spot C
The other weak spot is the phone line. Older systems called out over a landline. A storm can take that line down, and an intruder can disable it on purpose. A cellular communicator doesn't need the line. It's also the quickest, most reliable way to send a signal. An alarm is a link between your house and the help you need. So ask anyone selling you a system two things. Does the panel signal when the door opens, before anyone types a code? And does it call out over cellular?
