2 Fifteen videos in one afternoon Podcast set 74s

Fifteen videos in one afternoon sounds like a stunt. But nobody on my shoots works any faster than you do. They just quit filming one video at a time. Why pay for the setup fifteen times?

Most people film video one from start to finish. Then they move the camera, fix the light, and film video two. Every video gets its own setup, its own warm up, and its own ten minutes of trying to remember where they were. By video four they're cooked.

I've watched clients hit that wall right around lunch. So we flipped it. We mark three spots in the room before anybody shows up. Two framed shots and one plain spot with nothing behind you.

Every script gets chopped into short pieces, and each piece gets assigned to a spot. Now you're bouncing between three setups that are already lit and already framed. No moving lights. No resetting the camera.

On one shoot a real estate agent read fifteen scripts across twelve spots, and we found every single line on camera. Two hundred nine lines. That's what a plan buys you.

3 Your first line gives it away Podcast set 68s

Most people's first line tells you exactly what the video is about. Which sounds smart. Except it's the reason nobody stays. If I already know where you're going, why would I wait around to get there?

So every hook I write has three beats, and they happen fast. Beat one leans into something you already believe. Beat two is a single short line with a turn word in it. But, except, although.

Beat three snaps the other direction. That's the whole thing. Watch it work on something boring, like a plumber. "Everybody says to flush your water heater once a year."

"But that's not what kills it." "There's a part inside that tank nobody checks, and it's been eating the tank for years." Now you have to stay. The lean pulled you in, the turn caught you, and the snap opened a question you need answered.

The answer comes after that, and never in the first line. Rewrite your next opener with those three beats and read it out loud.

4 Your hook list expired Podcast set 73s

Somebody sends you a list of fifty viral hooks and you save it. Smart move, right? Except half of them were dead before you hit save. Hooks wear out.

"Wait for it" used to hold people. "Hey guys" used to be normal. "In today's video" used to be fine. Now the second somebody hears one of those, their thumb's already moving, because they've heard it ten thousand times.

A hook works because it's a little surprising, and nothing stays surprising once everybody's using it. So when did your list get written? Before I write scripts for anybody, I check what's working that week. TikTok has a Creative Center where you can see the top performing ads from the last seven days.

I skim the openers, I write down the patterns, and I cross off anything that's started to feel like a template. It takes fifteen minutes. It's the cheapest fifteen minutes in the whole process. Keep the list if you want it.

Just put a date on it. And the day you write, check it against what people are scrolling past right now.

5 You can't film that sentence Podcast set 69s

Every business I've ever filmed wants to say they care about their customers. And I believe them. But you can't point a camera at that sentence. What's anybody supposed to picture?

"We care about our customers" is a claim. Everybody makes it. The guy down the street who never calls you back makes it too. So when you say it, the viewer files it right next to his.

What they can picture is something you did. A trash hauler I film for missed a pickup one week. Their crew drove back out the same day and took it for free. Nobody asked them to.

That's a story. You can see the truck turning around. So before you write a single script, make a list of things you've done. The refund you didn't have to give.

The Saturday call you took. The job you redid because it bugged you. Every one of those is a video. The caring part, you'll never have to say out loud.

People watch it happen and decide for themselves.

6 Four tells your script came from AI Edit desk 72s

I use AI to help write scripts every week. And I can still spot an AI script in about four seconds. So can the people scrolling past yours. There are four tells, and they show up every time.

One is the setup and swap. "It's not about the camera." "It's about the story." That pattern shows up so often it's basically a watermark.

Two is the announcement. "Here's the thing." "Let me break it down." Real people just say the thing.

Three is no contractions. Nobody talks like "I do not think it is worth it." You'd say "I don't think it's worth it," and the first one sounds like a robot reading a hostage note. Four is the hardest to see.

Every script is shaped the same. Same length, same three points, same tidy wrap up line. Why does it matter if a viewer can't name any of that? Because they can feel it, and they leave.

On one batch I counted a hundred nineteen of these in thirteen thousand words, and we got it down to one.

7 I wrote thirty eight scripts and half got cut Edit desk 56s

I once wrote thirty eight scripts for a single shoot. I was proud of that number. Then nearly half of them got skipped in the room. The sentences were fine.

The problem was what each one leaned on. So what went wrong? I'd built the list by walking down an outline, one script per section. It looked organized on paper.

Which meant half of them only made sense if you'd seen the one before it. And nobody sees the one before it. Short videos show up alone, out of order, in front of a stranger. The client would read a few lines and say, "this one doesn't stand on its own."

And they were right every time. The next batch, I wrote twenty four. Every one was a complete idea a stranger could follow cold. All twenty four got shot.

8 Most of these videos won't work Edit desk 73s

Most of the videos on this account are going to underperform. I'm saying that up front, before any of them post. And I'm still going to post every one of them. Why would anybody plan to flop?

Because a new account doesn't know who it's for yet, and neither does the app. TikTok shows a new video to a small test group first. If that group sticks around and comments, it gets shown to more people. If they don't, it stops.

Early on, the app's still figuring out who your people are, so a lot of those test groups are the wrong people. That's why it's normal for most of your first twenty posts to miss. It's not a verdict on you. It's the app learning.

Where people go wrong is quitting at post six. Three flops in a row feels like proof. It's just data, and you don't have much of it yet. This is the whole reason I shoot a month at once.

When post six flops, post seven is already filmed. You can't quit on a video that's already done.

9 The boring shot that saves the edit Edit desk 68s

This is the most boring shot I'll film all day. Blank wall. Nothing behind me. And it's the one that saves the edit.

On every shoot I mark three spots. Two of them look good, with something in the background that says where you are. The third one's plain on purpose. When you flub a line at spot one, you don't start the whole video over.

You redo that one line at the plain spot, and in the edit it just looks like a camera change. Who's going to notice a cut to a wall? Nobody. That's the point.

A plain background fits next to anything, so it can patch any hole in any script. It also kills the worst habit people have on camera, which is trying to get ninety seconds perfect in one take. You can't. I can't.

Pros read off a prompter in short chunks and cut between angles, and the plain spot is what makes that cheap. Put yours somewhere you can step to in two seconds.

10 Treat shoot day like a paying client Edit desk 65s

If a client offered you three grand to show up Tuesday at nine, you'd be there at eight forty five. Rested. Dressed. Ready.

But put "film content" on that same Tuesday and it's the first thing to go. A customer calls, a supplier's late, and the shoot slides to next week. Then the week after that. So which one brings in more clients over a year?

Treat the shoot like that paying client booked it. Put it on the calendar and tell everybody who needs to know. Your team, your spouse, your kids. Sleep the night before.

Skip the greasy lunch in the middle of it. Have your caffeine ready before you start. And pick your outfits the night before, because standing in front of your closet at seven in the morning eats an hour you don't get back. It's like packing water before a hike.

You don't figure that out halfway up the mountain. Show up like it's paid, because it is.

11 Your script sounds like the evening news Window wall 58s

I once handed a client a batch of scripts that were clean, accurate, and kind of dead. Every sentence was correct. It still sounded like the evening news. So what was missing?

Questions. I went back and counted. Four question marks in over ten thousand words. That's somebody at a podium reading facts at you.

People don't talk like that. They ask you something, they guess what you're thinking, and they argue with themselves out loud. So we rewrote the batch with one rule. Every script had to ask the viewer something, and say the objection they were already thinking.

"Yeah, but I don't have time for that." Thirty one questions in the same length. Same facts, same client, mostly the same words. It finally sounded like a person talking to you.

Go count the question marks in your last script before you film it.

12 Keep the last take Window wall 65s

The shoot is the fun part. The edit is where most people's month of content goes to die. Because now you're staring at four hours of footage. Where do you even start?

I use one rule for every line of every script. Keep the last complete take. Delete everything before it. If you said a line three times, the third one's the keeper, because you only stopped once it felt right.

You don't rewatch takes one and two to compare. You don't agonize. That one rule turns editing into a search. Find the line, find the last good read, drop it in, and move on.

On a shoot this fall we pulled a hundred and two clips out of under twenty four minutes of footage. Sixteen scripts. None of it needed a debate. Slate it out loud while you shoot, say which script and which piece, and future you will find everything in seconds.

Then the evening after the shoot is just assembly.

14 Same shirt, every video Window wall 61s

Fifteen videos in the same shirt is a giveaway. You post one on Monday, one on Thursday, one the next Tuesday. And it's the same shirt in every single one. Does anybody notice that?

They do. Maybe not on purpose, but something feels off, like you're stuck on a loop. And on a new account, off is enough to get scrolled past. So I plan outfits before the shoot the same way I plan scripts.
