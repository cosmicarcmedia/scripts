1
My people came in the 1860s. I've sold fifty thousand acres since.
The origin and the face
▾
A land seller is worth more when the buyer knows whose family he is dealing with. This gives the face behind a six figure decision a long local history before anyone calls.
Spot A
I'm Mathew Gaines, and I grew up in Coleman, Texas. My people came to Central Texas in the 1860s. I'm the third generation in my family to sell real estate. [OWNER: what your father and grandfather sold, and where.] I went to school here in Coleman. After that I studied architectural drafting, which taught me to read a plat and a survey. That still comes in handy.
Spot B
In 1999 I went into ranch real estate full time. That's twenty seven years now. In that time I've sold more than fifty thousand acres. I don't list houses inside the city limits. Farms and ranches are what I do, and that's the only thing I do.
Spot C
I do it because I love the land. Ranching, hunting, fishing, and a dark night sky with a lot of stars in it. [OWNER: the first property you ever sold, and who bought it.] If you've got five acres to sell, I'll sit down with you. If you've got five thousand, I'll do the same. I'm in the office at 1820 South Commercial in Coleman, and you can call me at 325 625 9001. Gaines Land Sales has been selling Central and Central West Texas farms and ranches since 1999. If your family has owned that land a long time, I'd like to hear the story.
2
This county has been sold and cut up before
The local anchor
▾
Coleman County land has changed hands in waves, and a buyer or seller who hears that from a native knows they are talking to someone who understands the long view.
Spot C
Before 1857, almost nobody was ranching in Coleman County. Then Fort Colorado went up, and cattle started coming in. The early ranches were open range. There weren't any fences. John Chisum ran a big herd around Trickham before he took it on to New Mexico. That's how my kind of business began.
Spot A
Then around 1900, the big ranches started breaking up. They got cut into farm homesteads. Trainloads of families came into this county to buy a farm and plant cotton. Later, cattle took over as the main money crop. Oil and gas came along beside it. The county has been through several versions of itself, and the land has outlasted all of them.
Spot B
When I walk a place with a seller, I'm walking that history. An old homestead, a fence line, a tank somebody dug a hundred years ago. Every piece of dirt here has a story, and the story is part of the value. My family came in the 1860s, so I know some of these stories by name. [OWNER: one piece of land in Coleman County whose history you know by heart.] If you're thinking about selling, tell me what you know about your place. Somebody dug that tank, and I'd like to know who.
3
A website can post your ranch. It can't tell you what the creek does in May.
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Names no one, and gives the seller a simple reason to choose a local broker over an online listing: someone who has walked the ground and knows the neighbors.
Spot B
Anybody can put a ranch on a website. A listing shows the acres, the price, and a few photos taken on a nice day. Here's what it leaves out.
Spot C
It doesn't tell you where the water is, or what the creek does in May. It doesn't tell you which neighbor's cattle get through the fence. It doesn't tell you whether the road washes out, or who keeps it up. Those are the things a buyer finds out the hard way, a year after closing.
Spot A
I'm a full time broker, and I live here. I've walked these places. I know the roads and the neighbors. I'll tell a buyer what I know before the offer, because I'll see that buyer at the feed store for the next twenty years. The same goes for a seller. If your place has a problem, I'd rather hear it from you first and work out how to say it right. That's what makes a sale stick. [OWNER: a time knowing the neighbors or the road saved or sold a deal.] A website can't drive out there with you. It can't open the gate and walk the fence. Call me before you put a sign on the gate.
4
Five questions to ask before you buy land
The useful one
▾
Free, useful, and exactly what a first-time land buyer is afraid to ask. It earns the follow and shows the broker answers questions without a sales pitch.
Spot A
Thinking about buying a farm or a ranch? Ask these five questions before you sign anything. Where's the water? A well, a tank, a creek. Ask what each one does in a dry year. A tank that's full in May can be a mud flat by August.
Spot B
Do the minerals come with the land? A lot of the time, someone sold them off years ago. Ask. Ask in writing. Even if you never plan to drill, you want to know who controls what's under your feet. How do I get in? You want legal access to the property, a road you can drive on for as long as you own it.
Spot C
Where are the fences, compared to the property lines? Fences get moved. Get a survey, so you know what you're buying. A survey costs money, and it costs a lot less than a fight with a neighbor. And what's the land taxed as? A place used for agriculture can be taxed differently than one that isn't. Ask your title company how that works. That's the five. [OWNER: the question buyers never think to ask in Coleman County.] If you want to talk any of it through before you make an offer, call me. Gaines Land Sales, 325 625 9001. There's no charge for the conversation.
