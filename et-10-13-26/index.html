3
Five questions a listing photo will never answer
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
A photo shows a trailer and says nothing about its past. These five questions come straight from his own blog and hand the buyer a checklist worth forwarding, and they close on an offer to walk through any unit's history and paperwork.
Spot C
A used trailer can look perfect on the lot. A photo online looks perfect too. Neither one tells you what happened to it. So before you buy any used trailer, from me or from anybody else, ask five things. First, the title. You want it clean, with no liens. Ask how many owners it has had. A single long term owner usually comes with a more consistent story.
Spot A
Second, ask for the service records. Tire replacements, brake work, and any repairs to the frame or the flooring. A gap in the records does not rule a trailer out. Just know about it before you buy. Third, ask about accidents, and about any flooring or structural repairs. A trailer that was repaired the right way can still be a good buy.
Spot B
Fourth, ask how it was used. A trailer that made occasional local hauls has seen less wear than one that ran long distances or hauled commercially. Fifth, ask if any of the manufacturer warranty is left. Some warranties transfer to the next owner within a set window, and that coverage can be worth real money. On my lot, I am happy to walk through any unit's history and paperwork with you. Bring the five questions.
4
The walk-around before every haul
The useful one
▾
Lights, hitch, tires, bearings and brakes are what any trailer owner can check before pulling out, and his own service guide lists them. Giving the checklist away shows what the service department looks for and costs nothing.
Spot A
Before every haul, walk around your trailer. Start with the lights, since electrical trouble is one of the most common causes of problems on the road. Brake lights, turn signals and running lights. Then the hitch, the safety chains and the breakaway cable. Make sure all of it is secure before you pull out. Trailer tires often sit idle between trips, and that can cause dry rot or uneven wear that is easy to miss. Check the pressure every trip and look at the sidewalls for cracking.
Spot B
Wheel bearings need to be repacked or replaced on a regular schedule. If your trailer has electric brakes, the pads and magnets need a look now and then. Check the coupler and ball mount for wear, rust or looseness. Look under the trailer at the frame for rust, cracks and bent parts, especially after a heavy load over rough ground. Check the wiring and the plug for corrosion or fraying.
Spot C
If it is a horse trailer, look at the floor for soft spots, cracks or worn mats. Make sure the latches, hinges and dividers work. Latch the chest bars, butt bars and dividers before the truck moves an inch. Brake adjustments, bearing replacements and frame repairs are jobs for a technician. If you notice pulling, grinding, or a brake that responds a little different each time, bring it in before the next trip. [OWNER: how soon your service department can get a trailer in]
One more idea
The paperwork walk-through

Pick one used trailer on the lot and walk through its title, service records and warranty on camera, the way you would with a buyer. Open on the first question you ask about it. Blur or skip any private details.
