1
My grandmother started this company in 1960
The origin and the face
▾
The family name is the asset here. Three generations selling the same river country is the thing a land buyer in Menard County cannot get from a national listing site, and this puts a face on it.
Spot B
My grandmother started this company in 1960. Everybody called her Jody. She opened Menard Real Estate in a town of about nineteen hundred people. It has been in our family ever since. My parents are both licensed real estate agents, so real estate was the family business in every sense of the word. Jody Wright was my dad's mother, and her name is why I'm here.
Spot A
I was born in San Angelo, but I grew up here in Menard. Graduated from Menard High in 1995. Then Angelo State, a finance degree with a real estate option, finished in 2000. After that I came home to join the family business. [OWNER: what made you come back to Menard] I'm proud to carry it on. A lot of families around here have sold land for a generation or two. Ours has been doing it since 1960.
Spot C
Today we go by FINDARANCH.com, but it's the same business my grandmother opened. We sell river and ranch property here in Menard County and all over Texas. I'm Tyler Wright, third generation. If you're buying or selling land along the San Saba, there's a good chance my family has already been across it, and if you call, you're talking to the family that answers for it. Come see us.
2
Menard shortened its own name to get a railroad
The local anchor
▾
A piece of town history he can tell standing in the town itself. It is the kind of video a Menard native shares with 'I never knew that,' and every share reaches a ranch buyer's friends.
Spot C
This town used to have a longer name. When it was laid out in 1858, it was Menardville. Around 1910, the Fort Worth and Rio Grande Railroad asked the town to shorten it, so we did. The first train rolled in during 1911, and Menard boomed. Funny what a town will give up for a train.
Spot A
Before the railroad, the first county court here met under a live oak tree. That was 1871. A year later, they had a two story courthouse. By 1950, nearly twenty seven hundred people lived in Menard. These days it's about thirteen hundred, and we like it that way, mostly. It's the kind of place where people still wave from their trucks. The railroad stopped coming in 1972, and the old depot is now our county museum.
Spot B
But the river was here long before the train. In 1757 the Spanish built a mission and a presidio about a mile west of town, right on the San Saba. They dug an irrigation ditch to put river water on their fields. You can still see the ruins out on Highway 190. Every group that ever settled here picked this spot because of that river. Nobody has found a better reason yet. That's why my family has been selling land along it for over sixty years.
3
Ask your land broker how long they've known the river
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Gets forwarded because it gives a buyer three questions to ask any broker, and the answers favor a third generation local without naming a single competitor. His own testimonial supplies the proof.
Spot A
If you're shopping for river land, you'll meet a few brokers. A couple who bought through us told me they sat down with several before they found us. At our first meeting, they said, they knew. I want to hand you three questions so you can see why. You can use them on anybody, me included.
Spot B
First, ask how long they've been selling this stretch of country. Licenses are easy to count. Ask how long their family has known the river. Second, ask if you can call them from the road. My cell number is on our website, and I pick up. Third, ask how many places they're selling right now. [OWNER: how many listings you carry today] A short list means somebody knows every one of them by name.
Spot C
The big listing sites are good for finding a picture of a ranch. They can't tell you what the pecans along the bank look like in August, or how the road behaves after a rain. That comes from living here. My grandmother started this business in 1960. Ask me those three questions too. My grandmother started this in 1960, and the family is still here to answer. Then decide who you want walking your land with you.
4
What to check on river property before you fall for the view
The useful one
▾
Free advice a first time river land buyer needs and does not know to ask for. It positions him as the family that tells you the hard parts before the deal, and it earns the follow.
Spot B
Buying river property in the Hill Country is a different animal. The view sells it, so slow down and check four things. First, how much river frontage comes with it. Ask where the property line sits and get it on a survey. Second, ask what the river does in a wet year, and ask the neighbors, because they remember. They'll tell you things no listing sheet ever will.
Spot C
Third, how you get in and out. A low water crossing is great until it's under water. Fourth, look at what's growing on the bank. Here that's live oak, native grass, and a lot of pecan trees. They tell you a lot about how the ground handles water. A river tract is a long term investment for your family, so take your time. [OWNER: one river tip you only learn by selling on the San Saba]
Spot A
None of this takes special equipment. It takes a pickup, a day, and somebody who has walked it before. I've got a lot of family history in this country, and I'd rather you ask the hard question before you buy than call me about it after. I'm Tyler Wright with FINDARANCH.com in Menard, and I'm glad to walk it with you. Call anytime.
