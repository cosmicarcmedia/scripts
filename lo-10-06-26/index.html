Two things raise your chance of running into a side effect on testosterone therapy. High body fat, and insulin resistance. Why would either one matter? Start with body fat. Adipose tissue is metabolically active, and one of the things it does is express an enzyme called aromatase. Aromatase converts testosterone into estradiol. So the more adipose tissue you're carrying, the more aromatase activity there tends to be, and the more conversion is happening. Two people can get the same approach and end up in genuinely different places, because the tissue environment doing the converting is different. This one gets weaponized into an anti-estrogen argument, and I won't make that argument. Estradiol in men isn't the enemy, and I have a whole separate conversation about why blocking it aggressively is a mistake. Body composition changes how the therapy behaves. That's a mechanical fact about where you're starting from.

Then there's insulin resistance, where your cells stop responding to insulin as readily as they used to, so your body compensates by producing more of it. For a stretch, that works well enough that your fasting blood sugar can still look normal, which is part of why it develops quietly for years. Insulin resistance sits inside the same web as inflammation and hormone signaling, and those systems aren't independent of each other. So why does that matter for a man starting testosterone? A body running with meaningful insulin resistance is a different environment to introduce a hormone into than a body that's insulin sensitive, and you can change that environment.

The second reason is the one people feel, even if they can't name it. Missing foundations blunt your results. The most common disappointment I see in this space plays out almost identically every time. Someone starts therapy. Genuinely low, genuinely symptomatic, a real candidate. The first stretch is often good. Sleep improves, mood lifts, mental clarity comes back. They feel better than they have in years, and they're thrilled. Then it levels off. The changes they were most excited about, the way they wanted to look and feel physically, never quite arrive. They're better than they were, and short of what they pictured. And what do they conclude? Either the therapy stopped working, or the dose is wrong, or they need something added. So what happened? The therapy did its job, which was correcting a deficiency. The rest of the job belonged to something else that nobody did. That's a misattribution. You're blaming the treatment for a job it was never capable of doing by itself.

So what do I treat as non-negotiable alongside therapy? Four things. Number one, nutrient dense food. I'm deliberately not handing you a diet, because I don't think the specific template is the important variable, and I won't pretend to know which one fits your life. What I mean by nutrient dense is food that contains something, real protein, real produce, food that's recognizable as food. Protein earns a mention of its own, because if the goal involves building or keeping muscle, you need the raw material. Hormones don't manufacture tissue out of nothing. Number two, resistance training, at least twice a week. This is the one people skip, and it's the one most directly connected to what they want. It's also the single most misunderstood thing about this therapy. Testosterone doesn't build muscle on its own. Training builds muscle. So what do optimized hormones do? They can improve your capacity to train, and your capacity to recover so you can do it again. That's a genuinely valuable thing. If you've been too exhausted to train consistently for three years, getting that capacity back is a real win. But the capacity has to get spent, because capacity alone doesn't produce a body composition result.

Number three, zone two work. Zone two is easy aerobic effort, and the practical test is that you can hold a conversation while you're doing it. If you're gasping, you've gone too hard for this purpose. Inclined walking counts, and I'd argue it's the most underrated item on this entire list, because the barrier to entry is close to zero and it's the one people stick with. A treadmill at an incline, or a real hill, at a pace where you can still talk. That work is aimed at your metabolic machinery. It's a different stimulus than lifting, so it can't stand in for lifting. Number four, a regulated circadian rhythm and real sleep. I put this last on the list, and it may be the most important one on it. Almost every hormone we're discussing is sensitive to sleep and to light timing. A meaningful amount of hormonal and metabolic regulation happens during sleep, and short or disrupted sleep changes how your body handles blood sugar and appetite over time. Short sleep puts that regulation on a compressed, interrupted schedule. Practically, that's light in your eyes early in the day, less bright light late at night, and a schedule that stays roughly consistent.

So how do the two halves connect? They run as a loop, in both directions. Correcting a hormone deficiency can give someone back the capacity to do the work. Capacity to train, capacity to cook instead of ordering, capacity to hold a schedule together instead of surviving the day. Those are exactly the behaviors the foundations require, and they're the behaviors a genuinely deficient person often can't reach through effort alone. That's a real argument for therapy, and I make it regularly. Telling an exhausted person to simply try harder, when there's a correctable deficiency underneath the exhaustion, isn't good medicine. And it runs the other way too. Why does that direction matter just as much? The foundations determine how well the hormones work once they're on board, and how safely. Better insulin sensitivity, better sleep, lower inflammatory load, better body composition. All of that can make the hormone side more effective and more durable. Run either half alone and you get a predictable failure. Hormones alone gives you the person I described earlier, better for a while, then a plateau they can't explain. Foundations alone gives you someone doing genuinely hard work and pushing uphill the entire time, because a real deficiency is working against every bit of effort they put in. Sometimes for years, which is its own form of demoralizing. Neither of those people is doing anything wrong. They're each working one half of a connected system.

So, back to the question. Can you just take the testosterone and skip the rest? You can. People do it constantly, and it tends to go the same way. A higher chance of side effects on one side. A ceiling on results on the other. Then the therapy gets blamed for a job it was never going to finish alone. Nutrient dense food. Resistance training, at least twice a week. Easy aerobic work, and inclined walking counts. A real sleep and light schedule. What the right approach looks like for you depends on where you're starting, what your labs show, and what's going on in your life. This is the general shape of it, and your own provider is the person to build the plan with. The line I keep coming back to with patients is this. Hormones can give you the capacity to train. They won't do the training.
