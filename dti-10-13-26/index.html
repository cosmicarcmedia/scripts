The scripts

A first pass. I made some assumptions about your business to write these. On a shoot we'd dial them in to your goals.

1
Who is going to be holding your trophy
The origin and the face
▾
A hunter who is about to hand over the best animal of his life wants to know the person on the other side of the counter. This puts Gary's name, his 1979 shop, his scorer credentials and his refusal to take shortcuts into one minute, and it gives him a face to recognize before he ever calls.
Spot A
If you're about to hand a stranger the best animal you ever took, you ought to know who he is. I'm Gary Damuth, and this is Damuth Taxidermy in Brady, Texas. We've been making memories last since 1979. [OWNER: how you got into taxidermy, and the first animal you mounted for a customer] These days it's a thirteen thousand four hundred square foot shop with over twenty people in it. A whitetail from a lease down the road comes through the same doors as a lion or a leopard from the other side of the world.
Spot B
I've won awards for my work at the state level and the national level. [OWNER: the award you're proudest of, and the year you won it] I'm also a certified scorer for Pope and Young Club and Safari Club International. So when your animal lands on my bench, I know what a good one looks like, and I know what it measures. The shop belongs to the National Taxidermists Association and the Texas Taxidermists Association.
Spot C
The goal has always been simple. Be the best. That's why there are no shortcuts here, in the tanning, the mounting or the finishing. Every animal gets every step. The name on the sign is my name, so when a mount leaves this building, I'm the one who answers for it. African game and North American game, from kudu and lions to grizzly, whitetail, bobcats and mule deer. If it hangs on your wall, I want you proud of it every time you walk past it.
2
Friday afternoon on South Bridge Street
The local anchor
▾
Brady fills up with hunters every fall weekend, and this shop sits on the south end of town where they drive in. Naming that street, that traffic and that stretch of the year tells every hunter heading out to a lease that this video is for him.
Spot B
Friday afternoon in Brady, deer season. The trucks are rolling in off the highway. Folks fill up the tank, grab something to eat, buy groceries, and head out to ranches a lot of them have leased for ten or fifteen years. The game warden here told Texas Parks and Wildlife Magazine that traffic in town doubles. I don't need a magazine to tell me that. Brady is full of hunters heading to their weekend spots.
Spot A
We're on South Bridge Street, on the south end of town, right where the outfitters, the deer blinds and the game processing shops line up. Those trucks are headed out to hunt, and plenty of them come back with something to hang. [OWNER: what the first Monday after opening weekend looks like at the shop] Bring it by on the way home. If you live too far away to come back for it, we can ship the finished mount to you.
Spot C
People have been talking about big deer from this county since 1892. That's about when the Brady Buck came out of the country around here, roughly fifteen miles from town. It once held the world record for a nontypical whitetail, and the antlers are on display at the Buckhorn Saloon and Museum in San Antonio. If yours turns out to be the big one, we're at twenty three hundred South Bridge Street.
3
Ask any taxidermist how long it takes, and what happens in that time
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Every taxidermy shop can quote a price. Fewer will say a long number out loud, before the deposit. Putting fifteen to eighteen months up front, with the questions a hunter should ask any shop, turns the wait from the thing customers grumble about into the proof of the work.
Spot C
Before you leave a trophy with any taxidermist, ask one question. How long, from the day I pay the deposit? At Damuth Taxidermy, the goal is fifteen to eighteen months. That's a year and a quarter, up to a year and a half. I know that's a long time. I say it first, on purpose. [OWNER: what customers say when they hear fifteen to eighteen months, and what you tell them] Then ask for that number in writing.
Spot A
Here's where the time goes. We don't take shortcuts in the tanning, the mounting or the finishing. Every hide gets every step, and it takes the months it takes. [OWNER: what is happening to a mount in month four, month ten and month fifteen] One of my customers wrote that it was definitely worth the wait. Another said it took over a year and a half to get it back, and he's pleased with the outcome.
Spot B
Ask the next shop to show you a mount that's hung on somebody's wall for five or ten years, and look at how it's held up. Ask who tans the hide, and why they picked that tannery. [OWNER: where your hides are tanned, and why] Ask who is doing your animal, and whether he can score it. I'm a certified scorer for Pope and Young Club and Safari Club International, so I can. My customers will tell you we run a little higher on price. They'll tell you the work is lifelike, too. I'd like you to know all of that before you hand me the animal.
4
What to do in the first few hours so your deer makes a good mount
The useful one
▾
Hair slip and short capes ruin mounts before the animal ever reaches the shop, and every hunter who reads or hears the fix is a future customer who already trusts him. It costs Gary a minute to say, and it gives him a reason to be the first call this season.
Spot A
What you do in the first few hours decides whether I have a cape to work with. Heat is the enemy. Once a hide warms up, bacteria go to work, and the hair starts to slip. Once the hair slips, that cape is in real trouble. A deer that rides around for five or six days often loses its cape. Don't hang it to age unless it's in the shade and the temperature is in the forties or colder. [OWNER: how many hours you'd give a cape in a Texas October before you start to worry]
Spot B
When you gut it, stop the cut short of the front legs. Don't split the brisket. For the cape, cut around the body just behind the front legs, never in front of them. Leave me the whole cape. If it's cut too short, I can't put it back. If you leave too much, I'll trim it. Don't cut the throat. That's one of the surest ways to ruin a cape. [OWNER: the mistake you see most often when a cape comes through the door]
Spot C
Drag it by the front legs, and keep the head and shoulders off the dirt. Dragging it backward rubs the hair the wrong way. If it rides in the truck, put a tarp over it and keep it out of the sun. Skip the victory lap through town. Every stop is more handling on that hide. Get it cool, then get it frozen or get it to me as fast as you can. [OWNER: where a hunter can drop a deer at the shop, including weekends and after hours] If you've never caped a deer, leave the head alone and bring it to me.
