The One Page Nobody Reads (And The One That Decides Your Claim)

A walk through an actual policy, page by page, so you know what each section is for before you ever need it to matter.

Personal lines, policy literacy ~11 min runtime

You've had this policy for three years, maybe five, and you've read exactly one page of it. The first one. The rest went in a drawer, or a folder on your computer you've never opened twice. I'm with Texas Select Insurance here in San Angelo, and I'm not telling you that to make you feel bad about it. Almost nobody reads their policy. But here's what nobody tells you when they hand it to you. The page you did read isn't the page that decides your claim. It's the pages you didn't that do that. So today I'm going to open one up and walk it front to back with you, plain English, no jargon left unexplained, and by the end you'll be able to do this yourself with any policy you own. But I want to start with something that happened, because it's the whole reason this matters.

A guy calls us after a pipe bursts in his attic. Real damage, ceiling down in two rooms. He's certain he's covered, because he remembers his agent saying something about water damage years ago. Except the exclusion that applied wasn't about the pipe. It was about how long the water had been running before anyone noticed, and that language was sitting in a section he'd never once opened. That's the moment this stuff stops being paperwork and starts being money. So let's make sure it never happens to you.

Start with the declarations page, because that's the one page most people do see. Think of it as the cover sheet. It tells you who's insured, what dates your coverage runs, and it lists your coverages with a dollar limit next to each one. That limit is the most the policy will ever pay for that type of loss, full stop. You'll also see your deductible there, and your premium. If you only ever read one page, that's the one, but it's not the one that trips people up.

Behind it are five more sections, and every policy you'll ever own, home, auto, business, uses roughly the same structure. The insuring agreement is the company's promise in one paragraph, basically we'll pay for covered losses under these terms. Then definitions, which almost everyone skips and which is honestly the most useful section in the whole document. Any time you see a word in quotation marks inside your policy, that word has a specific legal meaning assigned to it back in definitions, and it's often narrower than what you'd assume walking in. Take the word occurrence, sitting in quotes on nearly every liability policy out there. Most people assume it means one single incident. But plenty of policies define it as continuous exposure to substantially the same harmful conditions, which can quietly turn what feels like ten separate incidents into one occurrence for the purpose of your limit. That's one sentence, buried in a section almost nobody opens, and it can be the difference between ten limits worth of protection and one. Then exclusions, which is where the company draws the line on what it won't touch. Then conditions, the rules both sides have to follow for the policy to function. And last, endorsements, the attachments that change your base coverage, add something, remove something, or adjust a limit.

Here's a question worth asking yourself right now. Do you know what your dwelling limit is, and do you know if it still matches what it would cost to rebuild your house today? Construction costs have moved a lot in the last few years, and a limit that was right when you bought the policy might be quietly wrong now. Most companies recommend insuring to full replacement cost, which is different from market value. It's what it would cost to build the exact same structure again, from the ground up, at today's prices.

Now, deductibles, because this is where I hear the most surprise from clients. Your deductible is what you pay before the insurance kicks in, and on home and auto it applies per claim, not once a year like health insurance. Two claims in a year means paying it twice. Here in Texas, a lot of home policies carry a separate deductible for wind and hail, and it's usually a percentage of your dwelling coverage, not a flat number. Two percent on a two hundred thousand dollar home is a four thousand dollar deductible, and you don't want to learn that number for the first time while you're standing in your driveway looking at a hail damaged roof. One more thing on this, and it matters, a contractor can't legally offer to eat your deductible for you here in Texas. If someone offers that, that's not a deal, that's a red flag.

Okay, exclusions, the section everybody skips and the one that comes up in almost every dispute I've ever seen. They're not tricks. They're the lines the policy draws around what it was built to cover. Standard Texas home insurance excludes flood, plainly, in writing, no fine print required to find it. It excludes earthquake too. But watch the wording closely, because sometimes an exclusion has an exception built into it, language like "except as provided by endorsement," and that word except is doing real work. It means there might be a way to buy the coverage back. Sewer backup is a good example, usually excluded in the base policy, usually available as an endorsement for a separate premium.

So walk your own risks against your own exclusions. What's likely to hit your specific property? Out here that's wind, hail, a hard freeze splitting a pipe, maybe flooding after a heavy rain. Check each one against what's excluded, and if something you're genuinely worried about is on that list, you have options, an endorsement, a separate policy, or at minimum you now know exactly where your exposure sits instead of finding out during a claim.

Endorsements themselves are just customization, nothing scary about the word. Some get attached automatically as part of your state's standard form. Others got added because you or your agent chose them. An ordinance or law endorsement, for instance, covers the extra cost of bringing an older home up to current code when you rebuild after a covered loss, and without it, upgraded electrical or plumbing required by your city might come out of your pocket. Scheduled personal property is another common one, listing specific high value items like jewelry individually so the standard sub limit doesn't cap what you'd actually recover.

Before we get to replacement cost, one more term worth knowing if you own a business or carry a bigger liability policy, per occurrence versus aggregate limits. A per occurrence limit caps what's paid for any single event. An aggregate limit caps what's paid across every claim during the entire policy period combined. Think of the aggregate as a bucket, every claim dips into it, and once that bucket's empty, the policy stops paying for the rest of the period, even if no single loss ever came close to the per occurrence number on its own. Personal auto and home policies mostly just use per occurrence language for liability. Commercial policies run both together, and if you're a business owner, that distinction is worth understanding well before a bad year of claims makes it matter.

Last piece, and it's the one that changes real dollar amounts more than anything else we've covered. Replacement cost versus actual cash value. Replacement cost pays what it costs to repair or replace your property today, full stop. Actual cash value pays that same number minus depreciation for age and wear. A ten year old roof that costs ten thousand to replace might only be worth seven thousand in actual cash value terms, and that three thousand dollar gap is yours to cover. Here's the part that catches people off guard even when they do have replacement cost coverage. A lot of policies pay you the depreciated amount first, then release the rest, called recoverable depreciation, only after you've finished the repair and shown proof. So the check doesn't always come the way people expect, and knowing that in advance saves a very confused phone call later.

At every renewal, give this ten minutes. Check your dec page against last year's. Ask why your premium changed if it did. Think about anything new in your life, a renovation, a home business, new jewelry or equipment, because standard policies often have thin or no coverage for business gear used out of your house. Check your dwelling limit against current rebuild cost. And if you had a claim, know that in Texas, three or more non weather claims in three years can give a company grounds to nonrenew you, though weather claims like hail are treated differently and can't be used the same way against you.

So here's what I want you to take from all of this. Your declarations page is your summary, start there. The six sections, dec page, insuring agreement, definitions, exclusions, conditions, endorsements, each does a specific job, and now you know what that job is. Limits cap what's paid. Deductibles are your share, and watch for percentage based wind and hail deductibles here in Texas. Exclusions tell you what's not covered, and endorsements can sometimes buy it back. And replacement cost versus actual cash value might be the single biggest swing factor in what you actually collect.

If you want to sit down with us and go through your actual policy, line by line, we'll do it, no charge, no pressure. That's not a sales pitch, that's just what the review is for. Because the worst time to learn what your policy says is the day you need it to say something specific, and that day doesn't send you a warning first.

02

What Happens After You Call In A Claim

Two real scenarios, a hailstorm and a wreck on Sherwood Way, tracked from the first phone call to the final check.

Claims process, home and auto ~10 min runtime

Most people file an insurance claim maybe three or four times in their entire life. Which means when it happens, almost nobody knows what they're doing, and it's usually happening on the worst day of their week. I'm with Texas Select Insurance in San Angelo, and I want to take the mystery out of this completely. We're going to follow two real situations, a hailstorm that tears up a roof and an accident on Sherwood Way, from the first phone call all the way to the final check. And I'm going to answer the question almost every single client asks me at some point, does filing this actually raise my rate. Stick around for that one, because the answer isn't what most people assume.

The first few hours after something happens matter more than people realize, and here's why. Before you clean anything up, before you touch a single thing, take photos and video. Every room, every angle, wide shots and close ups, the whole scene, not just the damage itself. If it's your car, walk all the way around it and get the impact points specifically. Your claim gets evaluated on documented evidence, and if you clean up first, that evidence is gone. Now, you're also required to prevent further damage where you reasonably can, so board up a broken window, tarp a torn roof if rain's coming, just keep every receipt for the materials, because that's reimbursable.

Once you've documented it, you call it in, and that call has a name, first notice of loss. Have your policy number ready, the date, the location, how they can reach you. You'll get a claim number back, write it down, you'll use it every single time you call after this. Texas gives you roughly a year to file a home claim from the date of the damage, but don't sit on it, the sooner you report, the sooner anything moves.

Here's Maria, hail tore through her roof and a couple of windows this spring. And here's David, someone ran a red light on Sherwood Way and hit his truck. Both of them call in the same day, both get a claim number, both get told to expect an adjuster. That's the next call you're going to get, and here's who's on the other end of it.

The adjuster works for the insurance company, and I want to be straight with you about what that means. They're not against you, but they're representing the company's interest in getting the number right by their standard. For most claims that's fine, most adjusters are thorough and fair. But if your loss is large or genuinely complicated, you can hire your own, called a public adjuster, licensed by the state, working for you instead of the carrier, and they take a percentage of the settlement for it. You don't need one for a straightforward claim. You might want one for a complicated, expensive one, and if you do go that route, verify their license through the Texas Department of Insurance first, they run a consumer help line for exactly that.

Somewhere in here you might get asked for a proof of loss, a signed statement listing what was damaged and what it's worth. Not every claim needs one, a simple fender bender usually doesn't, but a bigger home claim with personal property involved often does. Be specific, be honest, don't round numbers loosely, and if you have receipts or old photos of what got damaged, use them. This is the best argument for keeping a running home inventory before you ever need one.

Now, the deductible, and here's exactly where it lands. It comes off the top before the company pays anything. Say Maria's roof and window damage comes in at twelve thousand, and she's got a two percent wind and hail deductible on a two hundred thousand dollar home, that's four thousand dollars she owes first, meaning the company's actual payment is eight thousand. David's collision deductible is a thousand on a six thousand dollar repair, so the shop gets five thousand from the company and a thousand from David directly. People sometimes expect a check for the full estimate before the deductible comes out, and that's just not how the math works.

And this next part is where replacement cost really shows its teeth in a live claim. If Maria's roof is ten years old and the full replacement cost is twelve thousand, the company might only pay actual cash value up front, call it eight thousand, minus her four thousand deductible, so her first check is four thousand dollars. Once she replaces the roof and turns in the contractor's invoice, they release the held back difference, called recoverable depreciation, bringing her total up to the full replacement amount minus the deductible. So the payment shows up in two pieces, and if nobody told you that in advance, the first check can feel a lot smaller than you expected. If your policy is actual cash value only, there's no second check, that depreciated number is the final word.

If you've got a mortgage on the home, there's one more wrinkle worth knowing before it surprises you. The check for structural repairs typically gets made out to both you and your mortgage company, not just you. You'll need to endorse it and send it their way, and they release the funds as the repair work progresses. Once your lender has what they need from you, Texas guidance requires them to release that money within ten days, and if there's ever a dispute over those funds specifically, that's actually handled by the Texas Attorney General's office, not the insurance company.

Every so often you'll get a letter with reservation of rights at the top, and I want you to not panic when you see it. It just means the company is continuing to investigate while it sorts out a coverage question, it's not a denial, it's a pause with a flag on it. Call your agent, have them walk you through what it means for your specific situation.

Texas puts hard deadlines on all of this, and it's worth knowing them, because they protect you. Fifteen days for the company to acknowledge your claim and tell you what they need from you. Fifteen business days after they have everything to accept or deny it, with a possible forty five day extension if they explain why in writing. Five business days to pay you once they've agreed to. Miss that five day window after agreeing to pay, and you may have grounds to sue for the claim plus interest and attorney's fees.

So what happens if you just flat out disagree with the number? Start by talking to the adjuster directly, show them your contractor's estimate if it's higher, ask them to walk you through their math. A lot of this resolves right there. If it doesn't, and the disagreement is purely about dollar amount, not whether it's covered at all, most policies have a built in appraisal process, you hire an appraiser, the company hires one, the two of them agree on a neutral umpire, and the umpire's call is binding. If the fight is actually about coverage, whether it's covered at all, appraisal doesn't apply, and that's when you'd file a free complaint with the Texas Department of Insurance or talk to an attorney.

Now, the question everybody's been waiting for. Does filing a claim raise your rate? Here's the honest answer, it depends, and here's the actual nuance behind that. Texas law says three or more non weather claims in three years can give a company grounds to not renew you, and after two, they have to warn you before a third one counts against you. Weather claims, hail, wind, are treated differently and generally can't be used against you the same way. There's also a database called CLUE that tracks claims history on you and on the property for seven years, and companies pull it at renewal. But here's what I want you to hear. None of that should scare you off filing a real claim. The math on a five thousand dollar loss is different from the math on a fifty thousand dollar loss, and for anything significant, filing is almost always the right call. For something small, right around your deductible, that's worth a quick call to your agent first, just to think it through together.

So document everything before you touch anything. Get your claim number and keep notes on every call. Know your deductible ahead of time, especially that wind and hail percentage. Understand replacement cost usually pays in two pieces. And on the rate question, weather claims sit differently than everything else, and real losses are worth filing regardless.

If you're in the middle of a claim right now and something about it doesn't feel right, call us. We're right here in San Angelo, and this is exactly the kind of thing we help people through every week.

The Life Insurance Conversation Nobody Wants To Have Honestly

Term versus permanent, how much coverage actually makes sense, and the beneficiary mistake that costs families the most.

Life insurance, personal lines ~10 min runtime

Life insurance might be the most oversold, least understood product in this entire industry, so let's just skip the pitch part entirely. I'm with Texas Select Insurance here in San Angelo, and today I'm giving you the honest version, no pressure, nothing to sign at the end. How it works, what term and permanent really do, how to think about how much you need, and the handful of mistakes I watch cost families real money.

Here's the core of it, stripped all the way down. You pay a premium, the company agrees to pay a lump sum, called a death benefit, to whoever you name, called a beneficiary, if you die while the policy's active. That's the entire mechanism. And here's the thing worth sitting with for a second, this product doesn't actually benefit you. It benefits the people who depend on you. Which is honestly the healthiest way to think about it, because it moves the question away from what do I get and toward what does my family need if I'm not here.

So, term life first, because it's the simpler of the two, and it's where most people should start. You pick a length, ten years, twenty, thirty, and you pay a level premium for that entire window. Die during the term, your family gets the death benefit. Outlive the term, the policy just ends, no payout, it did its job by existing. Most term policies lock your rate at the age and health you were when you bought it, so a thirty five year old buying a twenty year term keeps that same rate until they're fifty five. Renew after that and you're priced on your age at renewal, not your original age, which is exactly why buying long when you're young tends to make more sense if you'll need the coverage a while. Most term policies also carry something called a convertibility feature, meaning you can convert it to a permanent policy down the road without another medical exam, even if your health's changed in the meantime. That one feature can matter a lot more than it sounds like it does the day you buy the policy, especially if your health takes a turn you didn't see coming. Your actual premium gets built from a handful of factors, your age, your health class, whether you use tobacco, and the amount of coverage you're buying. Two people the same age can land in completely different rate classes depending on health, and that's exactly why waiting doesn't just cost you time, it can cost you the rate class you'd have qualified for today.

Permanent life is a different animal, whole life and universal life being the two common versions, and it's built to last your entire life, not a set window. Part of every premium goes toward the insurance itself, part goes into something called cash value, basically a savings account living inside the policy that grows over time. Now here's where I see people get confused, and it's worth being precise about. Cash value isn't the death benefit. They're two separate pots. Die with a whole life policy, your beneficiary gets the death benefit, the cash value doesn't stack on top of it. You can borrow against the cash value, or surrender the policy and take what's built up, but it takes years to become meaningful, and the premiums run considerably higher than term because you're funding both pieces at once. Universal life is the more flexible cousin, letting you adjust your premium and your death benefit within limits as your situation changes. That flexibility sounds appealing, but it comes with a real responsibility attached. If your premium payments aren't enough to cover the actual cost of insurance in a given year, the shortfall gets pulled from the cash value automatically. Let that cash value run dry without noticing, and the policy can lapse entirely, which is exactly why a universal life policy needs a periodic checkup to make sure it's still on track.

So how much do you actually need? I want to be straight with you, anyone who hands you a precise number without knowing your life is guessing. What I can give you is a framework instead of a formula. Start with income replacement, how many years would your family need your income covered, until the youngest is grown, until your spouse hits retirement. Add your actual debts, the mortgage balance, car loans, whatever's outstanding. Add final expenses, funeral costs run higher than people expect. Some people also add a rider here, waiver of premium keeps the policy active without payment if you become disabled, an accidental death benefit adds a payout on top for specific circumstances. Riders aren't required, and not every family needs one, but they're worth asking about once you know your base coverage number. Then subtract what you've already got, savings, retirement accounts, existing coverage, a spouse's income. The gap between those two numbers is the conversation worth having. Let's run one, so this isn't just theory. Say a family needs eight years of income replacement at sixty thousand a year, that's four hundred eighty thousand right there. Add a two hundred thousand dollar mortgage balance and twenty thousand in final expenses, and the total need lands around seven hundred thousand. Now subtract eighty thousand in existing savings and a hundred thousand in employer coverage already in place, and the actual gap left to fill is around five hundred twenty thousand. That's the number worth pricing a term policy against, not a guess pulled from a multiple of salary.

Match your term length to your actual exposure window, not to whatever the cheapest quote happens to be. Three year old at home, twenty years covers you through college. Just signed a thirty year mortgage, some people run the term that long to match it. Closer to retirement, a shorter term might be all you need.

Now let's talk about beneficiaries, because this is where I see the most heartbreaking mistakes, and they're completely avoidable. Life insurance pays out directly to whoever's named on the policy, and it bypasses your will entirely. Your will doesn't override that beneficiary form, ever. So here's what happens out there. Someone names an ex spouse in their twenties, gets remarried, has kids, and never once goes back and updates the paperwork. Divorce, remarriage, new kids, the death of an existing beneficiary, any one of those should trigger you pulling out that form and checking it. And second, your beneficiary has to know the policy exists to file a claim on it, the money doesn't show up automatically, so tell the people who need to know. There's a national resource for this exact problem, the NAIC runs a Life Insurance Policy Locator service that helps families search for policies after someone's passed and nobody's sure what exists. It's a good backstop, but it's still just a backstop, the real fix is a conversation you have while everyone's still around to have it.

Let me walk through the mistakes I see most often, because knowing them ahead of time is the whole point of this video. Relying only on your employer's group coverage, which usually caps out around one or two times your salary and disappears entirely the day you leave that job. Underinsuring because the premium felt high, which just means you paid for something that won't cover what your family needs when it matters. Waiting until you feel ready, except premiums climb with age and health changes can close doors that were open last year, someone insurable at thirty two can develop a condition by forty two that changes their options entirely. And forgetting to update beneficiaries, which we already covered, but it's worth saying twice because it's the one I see most.

So here's the honest summary. Term is straightforward, affordable coverage for a defined window, and it's the right fit for most people in their prime earning years with a family depending on that income. Permanent life is more complex, more expensive, and it has real uses, but it's not automatically the better option just because it costs more. How much you need depends entirely on your actual numbers, not a generic multiple of your salary. And the mistakes that cost families the most are all avoidable, if someone tells you about them before they happen instead of after.

I'm not here to tell you everyone needs a policy, or that bigger is always better, or that one type beats the other for every family. What I'm telling you is that if people depend on your income, this is a conversation worth having clearly, and honestly, while you still have the choice in front of you.

If you want to sit down and think through your actual picture, we're here, no pitch, just the numbers laid out plainly, whenever you're ready for that conversation.

Why A Standard Policy Can't Touch An Oilfield Claim

What operators, drilling contractors, and haulers across the Permian actually need beyond a standard business policy.

Energy, oil and gas coverage ~10 min runtime

Out on a lease, one piece of equipment can be worth more than the house you go home to at night. And one bad afternoon on that same location can turn into a claim that follows a company for years. I'm with Texas Select Insurance in San Angelo, and I work with operators and contractors across the Permian Basin and the Concho Valley. So let's build this whole picture from the ground up, who needs specialized coverage out here, what the core policies really do, and how the paperwork, the MSAs and the certificates, ends up mattering just as much as the coverage itself.

Almost everyone touching an oilfield operation needs something beyond a standard business policy, and here's why that's true across the board. Operators hold the lease and answer for everything happening on it. Drilling contractors run the rigs under high pressure, often right next to other infrastructure. Service contractors, your cementing crews, wireline, pressure pumping, completions, are on a different location almost every day, for a different operator each time. Haulers move crude, saltwater, produced water, sitting right at the intersection of energy risk and trucking risk, needing both done right. And even suppliers dropping off pipe or chemicals can carry contractual insurance requirements the moment their equipment sits on that pad. A policy built for a retail shop or a plumber was never built for any of this.

Start with general liability, the backbone every business carries in some form. It covers bodily injury and property damage your operations cause to someone else, a vendor falling at your shop, your equipment damaging someone's truck on location. Here's the gap that matters most in energy specifically. Most standard general liability policies carry an absolute pollution exclusion, and carriers often argue that language covers oil, gas, produced water, and drilling fluids. Which means the exact thing you're most exposed to out here might be the exact thing your base policy won't touch. The other gap worth knowing about sits inside completed operations specifically. If a service company finishes a job on a well and something goes wrong weeks or months later that traces back to that work, completed operations coverage inside the general liability policy is what responds, assuming the limits actually hold up. A million per occurrence used to be a reasonable starting point, but plenty of operators and MSAs now ask for two million or more, so check what your actual contracts require before assuming your existing limit clears the bar.

That's why pollution liability exists as its own separate policy in this industry, and it's worth understanding precisely what it does. Say a hose fails while a service company's pumping chemicals into a well, product hits the soil, leaches down, and now you're looking at environmental remediation. That cost, and any third party claims tied to it, falls under pollution liability, not general liability. Service companies working multiple sites usually want a contractor's pollution form that travels with them job to job. Operators tied to one location often want a site specific version, sometimes both. There's also a distinction between sudden releases and gradual ones, and it matters more than people expect. A hose failure is sudden, obvious, easy to point to. Slow seepage from an aging pipeline is gradual, and it might not show up as a visible event until well after it started. Some pollution forms cover both, some only cover sudden and accidental releases, and that difference is exactly the kind of thing worth confirming before you assume you're covered for whichever version happens to you.

Then there's control of well, built for one specific nightmare, a blowout, a kick, a crater. Regaining control of a wild well requires specialized firefighting crews, and their services run into the millions before the well's even back under control. This coverage picks up those costs, plus re-drilling if it comes to that, plus the pollution cleanup from the event itself.

Physical equipment out here takes a beating in the field, away from any fixed location, and that's exactly where standard commercial property stops being enough. Inland marine covers your tools and equipment while they're in transit, staged in a yard, or sitting on location, filling the gap a property policy leaves wide open. The thing worth checking, does your property policy's off premises limit match your real exposure, and does your inland marine pick up cleanly where it stops, because that seam is exactly where I've seen programs quietly fail.

Now, here's something that surprises people who aren't from here. Texas is one of the only states where workers compensation isn't legally required for most private employers. But being a nonsubscriber, the Texas term for skipping it, means you lose common law defenses if an employee gets hurt, and they can sue you directly. In an industry with real physical hazards, that exposure isn't theoretical, it's Tuesday. Most operators carry it anyway, and most MSAs require it regardless of what state law says. Workers comp in this industry gets rated using classification codes tied to the actual work being done, drilling crews, well service hands, pipeline workers, and shop employees each carry different rates built on historical loss data for that specific job type. Get someone misclassified, in either direction, and you're either sitting on a coverage gap or overpaying for a risk you don't actually carry, and either one tends to surface at the worst possible time, during an audit or right after a claim.

Commercial auto out here means pickups doubling as office space, service trucks hauling tools, tankers moving crude and chemicals down lease roads with no shoulders. A policy written for a florist's delivery van isn't built for that, and it shows the moment something happens on caliche instead of pavement. Make sure your commercial auto extends to hired and non owned vehicles too, meaning rental trucks and employee owned vehicles pressed into company use, because that's a gap that surfaces constantly in field operations where someone grabs whatever truck is closest to get to a location fast.

Above all of it sits your umbrella, picking up once your underlying limits run out. In energy, five million, ten million, sometimes more isn't unusual, because a single blowout or a serious road accident involving multiple parties can blow straight through a standard limit in one event. If your umbrella number was set five years ago and you haven't looked at it since, that's worth a real conversation, because MSA requirements have been climbing. You'll also hear the term excess liability used alongside umbrella, and while they get treated as interchangeable in casual conversation, they're not always the same thing on paper. An umbrella typically sits broadly above every underlying policy at once, where an excess layer can be written to follow just one specific policy beneath it, so ask your agent to walk you through exactly how yours is structured rather than assuming either term means the other.

Which brings me to the part of this video that matters most on a practical, this week, this contract level. Operators almost universally require an MSA before a contractor sets foot on location, and that agreement decides who's responsible for what when something goes wrong. Texas limits some of what these agreements can demand, the Oilfield Anti-Indemnity Act restricts pushing liability onto one party for the other party's own negligence. But the coverage requirements inside the MSA are real and specific, two million general liability, a million in pollution, statutory workers comp, whatever the contract says.

So when a contractor shows up, the operator needs proof, and that proof is the certificate of insurance, the COI. It's a snapshot, carrier, policy numbers, limits, dates. Often the MSA also requires the operator be named additional insured on the contractor's policy, giving them standing to claim under it if something tied to that work goes wrong. Here's what actually happens when this goes sideways, a contractor shows up with a certificate listing the wrong limits, or an exclusion the MSA doesn't allow for, and now they're either getting sent home off the job, or worse, personally exposed for a loss the insurance was supposed to have handled. That's not a paperwork problem anymore at that point, that's a very expensive afternoon.

So review your MSAs against your actual coverage at least once a year, because contracts change, operations change, and the two need to stay lined up on purpose, not by accident.

Not every carrier writes this business, and not every agent understands where the traps sit. A general commercial agent who mostly writes retailers and contractors can build you a program that reads fine on paper and has a hole in it the moment an actual oilfield claim hits. That's where the coinsurance clause comes in, and it's probably the single most misunderstood piece of commercial property insurance, so let's make it concrete with real numbers. Most policies require you to carry coverage equal to a minimum percentage of your building's actual replacement cost, commonly eighty percent. Say your building's true replacement cost is a million dollars, so you're supposed to be carrying at least eight hundred thousand. But your policy limit sits at six hundred thousand, set years ago and never revisited. A storm hits, and you've got two hundred thousand dollars in real, covered damage. Here's what actually happens next. The company divides what you carried, six hundred thousand, by what you should have carried, eight hundred thousand, and gets seventy five percent. The remaining fifty thousand is yours, because the clause treats the underinsured gap as if you'd chosen to self insure that portion. Now, replacement cost versus actual cash value plays into this same claim. Replacement cost pays current prices with no deduction for age. Actual cash value runs cheaper, and for some operations that's a legitimate budget call, but go into it knowing the gap can be significant, especially on an older building.

Business interruption deserves one more sentence, because the number people underestimate isn't the payout, it's the waiting period. Lost net income, ongoing payroll, rent you still owe even with the doors closed, all of it can be included, but only if the limit and the duration you picked actually match how long a real rebuild would take for your specific building.
