1
Thirteen years in other people's shops before I opened mine
The origin and the face
▾
Nobody pulling a rig into a commercial diesel shop knows the owner spent thirteen years in dealer shops first, or that he and his wife built the business from a 3,000 square foot building. That path is the reason a fleet manager can trust the name on the sign.
Spot A
Thirteen years. That's how long I turned wrenches in other people's shops. I started in a dealer shop in 1998 and stayed four years. In 2002 I moved to another dealership, and I stayed there nine. I learned a lot in both places. [OWNER: the one thing you saw in those shops that you promised yourself you'd do differently] So in 2011, I went out on my own.
Spot B
Reyes Diesel Service started as me and my wife Patricia. Our first building was three thousand square feet, and it wasn't in the best spot in town. That meant we had to be worth the drive. Every customer we had, we earned one truck at a time. [OWNER: the first customer who trusted you with a truck] Years later we outgrew that shop, and an SBA loan helped us build a new one.
Spot C
Today that's eight bays and twelve thousand square feet on Gibbs Drive. Engine-in-frames, transmission rebuilds, clutches, trailers, computer diagnostics. There's a showroom floor of parts, oils and lubricants now too. Over twenty years as a mechanic, and my name is still on the sign. We've been BBB accredited since 2015, with an A plus rating. A truck that's down is a paycheck that's down. You bring us a rig that's stuck, and we work to get it back on the road. If you drive for a living, this shop was built for you. Reyes Diesel Service. Gibbs Drive, San Angelo.
2
School buses, chemical rigs, and truckers from a hundred miles out
The local anchor
▾
The shop works on school buses for the City of San Angelo and nearby districts, rigs for one of the biggest chemical companies in the country, and truckers within a hundred miles, and nobody in town knows it. Naming those customers tells every fleet manager in the Concho Valley this shop is built for them.
Spot B
Take a look at what's parked outside our eight bays. School buses for the City of San Angelo and the districts around it. Rigs that haul for one of the biggest chemical companies in the country. Truckers who drove in from a hundred miles away. Different trucks, but when one goes down the problem is the same. A bus that can't run is a route that doesn't get driven. A rig that can't run is a driver who doesn't get paid.
Spot C
We built the new shop close to the truck stops by the highway junction. That's one of the best things about the move. When a truck breaks down, you want a shop that's easy to get to. [OWNER: which highway you sit closest to, and how many minutes it is from the truck stops] We're on Gibbs Drive, close to the middle of town. We're open Monday through Friday, eight to five. Past five, we do twenty-four hour emergency service.
Spot A
A few years back, Patricia and I entered the San Angelo Business Plan Competition. In 2022 we took second place. Twenty thousand dollars. That money was meant for one thing, more parts on the shelf and a system to keep track of them. The idea was simple. Fewer days waiting on a part means fewer days a truck sits. We've got a showroom floor now with parts, oils, lubricants and lighting. Come by and see it.
3
Three questions to ask any diesel shop before you hand over the keys
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
The shop has real answers to three things every fleet manager and owner-operator wonders about: who is under the truck, how long it waits on a part, and how a big bill gets paid. Laid out as questions to ask any shop, it draws a sharp line without naming anyone.
Spot C
Before you hand your keys to any diesel shop, ask them three questions. I'll answer all three for mine, and you can ask the next shop theirs. Question one. Who is going to be under my truck? At my shop it's mechanics with over twenty years of experience, and I'm one of them. Ask the next shop who's going to be under your truck, and how long they've been doing it.
Spot A
Question two. How long will my truck sit waiting on a part? We took second place in a business plan contest, and that prize money was meant for parts stock and an inventory system. We keep a showroom floor of parts, oils and lubricants right here in the shop. Fewer days waiting on a part means more days you're hauling. [OWNER: a part or two you keep on the shelf that saves a customer a day]
Spot B
Question three. What if I can't pay for the whole repair today? A big repair bill shows up at the worst possible time. We offer financing up to ten thousand dollars on repairs. You pick when and how you pay it back, and you hear a yes or no in seconds. So the truck gets fixed and gets back to work, and the payment gets figured out after. Those are the three. Ask the next shop the same ones. If you want to hear how we answer them in person, we're on Gibbs Drive, Monday through Friday.
4
What the code on your dash is trying to tell you
The useful one
▾
Every driver gets a warning light eventually, and most either ignore it or start guessing at parts. Explaining what a fault code is and what to tell a mechanic costs the shop nothing and shows the customer who reads codes for a living.
Spot A
When a warning light comes on, most drivers do one of two things. They ignore it, or they start guessing at parts. Either one can cost you money. The truck's computer writes down a code the second it sees something wrong. That code is where every diagnosis starts. It's what computer diagnostics are for. We read the code, then find out what's behind it.
Spot B
A code tells you which system is complaining, or which reading is out of range. Finding the broken part is the mechanic's job. That's why the part somebody told you to replace sometimes turns out to be fine. Two things help me a lot when a driver brings a truck in. Write down the code before anybody clears it. And tell me what the truck was doing when the light came on. Hauling a load, idling, climbing a grade. Cummins, Detroit, Cat, a DT 466, the code is where we start on all of them.
Spot C
The cheap stuff matters too. Oil changes, filters, batteries. That's preventative maintenance, and it's on our list for a reason. A dead battery on a Monday morning costs a lot more than the battery. If the light is on and you're unsure what to do, call us. We do twenty-four hour emergency service, and a phone call costs you nothing.
