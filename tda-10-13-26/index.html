1
I started dancing at three, in my mother's studio
The origin and the face
▾
Parents hand their child to a person, and this tells them who Stacia is: a San Angelo native whose mother ran a studio in town for thirty plus years, and who came home and opened her own in 2008. It is the strongest story on her website and few parents read that far.
Spot A
I was three years old the first time I walked into a dance studio. It was my mother's. Patricia Aylor School of Dance, right here in San Angelo. My mother ran a studio in this town for more than thirty years. So when someone asks how long I've been around dance, the honest answer is that I can't remember a time before it. I trained in ballet, tap, jazz, and acrobatics.
Spot B
When I was twelve, I saw my first Nutcracker in San Angelo. I auditioned the next year, and then I performed in every production for six years. I went on to Texas Tech and earned my degree in dance. I was a finalist for the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders, twice. I taught dance and gymnastics in Beaumont, and once I was a mother, group fitness and personal training. In 2007, I brought my two girls home to San Angelo.
Spot C
In 2008, with my children and my family behind me, I opened The Dance Academy. Today, several of the teachers you'll meet in this building started here as three and four year olds. Now they teach the next group of kids. [OWNER: one story about a former student who came back to teach] I'm Stacia. I own this studio and I still teach in it, right here on North Chadbourne Street.
2
Where a San Angelo dancer goes next
The local anchor
▾
Ties the studio to things San Angelo parents already know: the Tex-Anns, Dancing with the San Angelo Stars and the Symphony Belles and Beaux. A child who starts at three can see how far the road goes in this town.
Spot B
If you grew up in San Angelo, you know what a Tex-Ann is. I was one. A member my sophomore year, Second Lieutenant my junior year, and Co-Captain my senior year. So Drill Team Prep, for eighth grade and up, is personal to me. One of our teachers was a Tex-Ann for three years and Captain her senior year at Central High. She's in her fourth year teaching here while she's at Angelo State.
Spot A
We're at 57 North Chadbourne Street, downtown San Angelo. Every year, I dance as a Pro in Dancing with the San Angelo Stars. It raises money for Hospice of San Angelo, and I've done it for about nine years. I also choreograph the Father Daughter and Mother Son dances for the Symphony Belles and Beaux, and I volunteer my time to do it. So the person teaching your child is out there dancing in San Angelo too.
Spot C
Intro to Dance is for ages three to five. Levels One through Five run from age six to eighteen. A kid who starts at three can see where the road goes, from the Nutcracker to the drill team to Dancing with the San Angelo Stars. [OWNER: a former student who went on to dance somewhere in town] Some of our teachers started here at three or four years old, and now they're the ones at the front of the room. I'm Stacia. I started in a studio like this one, when I was three.
3
Before you pick a dance studio, ask who decides the level
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Most parents compare dance studios on price and schedule. This hands them better questions and answers each one from the studio's own placement, pricing and policy pages, without naming anyone. It is the one a parent forwards to another parent.
Spot C
Before you sign a child up for dance anywhere, ask two questions. Who decides which level my kid goes in? And what does your price pay for? Here's how we answer. A new dancer with no experience starts in Level One. A teacher evaluates them, and if the level is wrong, we move them. Intro to Dance, ages three to five, stays together as one class for the whole dance year.
Spot A
Now the price. It's on our pricing page that The Dance Academy is a for profit business. We get no city funding, no donations, no property tax discount. What you pay reflects my education and certifications, my instructors' experience, and what it costs to run the studio. Pay online and there's a three percent convenience fee. We say so in writing. Pay with cash or a check at the office and there's no fee.
Spot B
Last question. What are the rules for the grown ups? Our bullying policy covers every staff member, every dancer, and every parent, and we ask all of you to read it. Poise Dance Company, our competitive team, wants the basics of ballet and jazz before you audition, and at least six hours a week once you're on it. All of that is written down on our site, so you can read it before you ever call.
4
What to pack for your three year old's first dance class
The useful one
▾
Practical and sourced entirely from the dress code page most parents never open. It keeps a first day from going sideways and shows a new family what the studio expects before they walk in.
Spot A
If your three year old has a first dance class coming up, pack the bag before you do anything else. Intro to Dance is for ages three to five. They need a dance bag with a change of dance shoes in it. Then a zip lock bag with a full change of clothes, for accidents. Don't skip that one. Dance shoes stay inside, so they don't wear them out the door.
Spot B
Hair gets pulled back neatly, out of the face. Put extra elastics and a brush in the bag. We sell elastics at the office if you forget. If your dancer is here more than an hour, send a water bottle. No iced drinks. If it's more than two hours, a small snack that isn't messy is fine.
Spot C
One rule matters for the little ones. If your child needs help in the restroom, wait in the lobby during class. The teachers can't leave the room. And email before you pay. Some classes are full, so ask what's open first. The studio opens fifteen minutes before the first class, so there's time to get everyone settled. Your dancer stays in that Intro class for the whole dance year, so it helps to start in the right one.
One more idea
What happens in a first class

With a parent's permission, film the first few minutes of an Intro to Dance class from the moment the kids walk in: bags down, shoes on, hair back, then the first count. Say the expectations out loud as they happen, in the words Devin F. used. A new parent sees the structure before they ever call.
