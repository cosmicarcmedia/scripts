10 You can own the property and not what is under it Neutral wall 99s
On screenYou can own it. Not what's under it.

Everybody has watched a show about West Texas oil by now.

Here is the part those shows skip right past.

In Texas you can own the house, the fence, the driveway and every square inch of the lot, and still not own what is underneath it.

The mineral estate can be split off from the surface and sold separately, and once that happens the two travel independently forever.

Here is the part that surprises people.

The law treats the mineral estate as the dominant one.

Not equal to the surface. Dominant.

Which means whoever owns the minerals under your yard has an implied legal right to come onto your surface and go get them, and they do not need your permission to do it.

There are real limits on that.

They have to give you notice before they drill a new well.

They cannot be negligent about how they do it.

And there is a doctrine that protects you when they have a reasonable alternative and you genuinely do not.

But the starting point is nothing like what most buyers picture when they hear the word own.

So when you are under contract, two things.

Read the deed for reservation language, the phrase you are looking for is something like reserving all oil, gas and other minerals.

And read Schedule B of your title commitment, because if the minerals got severed off this property at any point in the last hundred years, that is where it shows up.

11 Ron Swanson would move here for the water law Neutral wall 100s
On screenRon Swanson would move here for the water law.

Ron Swanson spent seven seasons arguing that the government should have as little to do with your property as humanly possible.

I think about that man every time I explain Texas groundwater to somebody moving here from out of state.

Because in Texas, the water underneath your land belongs to you.

Not the state. You.

You can pump it, sell it, lease it, or haul it off the property in a truck.

It is called the rule of capture, it goes back to a court decision in 1904, and people around here call it the law of the biggest pump.

That is the part Ron would like considerably less.

Because your neighbor's water is his in exactly the same way, and he can pump as much of it as he wants.

If he drills deeper than you and pumps harder than you and your well runs dry as a result, in Texas that is generally his water now.

He does not owe you anything, unless he did it out of spite, or he is wasting it, or he was plain negligent about it.

That is the default rule.

What keeps it from turning into total chaos is the local groundwater district, which sets well spacing and permitting, and the one covering this county is headquartered out in Wall.

So if you are buying a place with a well, find out how deep yours goes and what the wells around it are doing.

The water is yours.

So is the risk.

That is a real piece of infrastructure that most people here have never thought about once.

On top of that the city draws on six different surface water sources, including Twin Buttes, O.C. Fisher and Nasworthy, because in this part of Texas you do not get to rely on any single one of them.

Meanwhile a well drilled out in the county is most likely pulling from the Edwards-Trinity, which is a completely different aquifer sitting under a big stretch of West Texas.

Same general part of the state, two entirely different water stories, two entirely different sets of problems.

So when somebody tells you country water and city water are basically the same deal, they are not.

And which one a house is on is worth knowing before you buy it, not after.
