I moved home to Ballinger in 1994
The origin and the face
▾
People in a town of three thousand four hundred buy from the face they know, and Mark has a real one: a son who came home to the family agency and later bought it. This puts the name behind the name on the door.
Spot A
In 1994 I moved home to Ballinger. [OWNER: where you were living and what you were doing before you came back] I came home to join my father in the agency he ran. This agency had been serving Ballinger since 1903. That's ninety one years of history by the time I walked in the door. I bought the agency from him in 2007.

The website says a hundred and twenty years. Here's who has been doing the work. Debbie has been here thirty years, every one of them with this agency. Terilyn has been here twenty. 2
Ballinger sold half its lots in a single day
The local anchor
▾
Everybody in Ballinger knows the town started with the railroad, but few can tell the story with the numbers. A short history lesson from the agency that has been here since 1903 makes Mark the person who knows the town.
Spot B
Ballinger exists because of its water supply. In 1886 the railroad was pushing west from Brownwood and needed a place to stop. Runnels City, five miles north, was the county seat then. Ballinger had better water, so the railroad picked us. That one decision is the reason there is a Ballinger on the map.
Spot C
The first lot sale was June 29, 1886. About six thousand people showed up. Roughly half the lots were gone by the end of the day. The town was first called Gresham, and then Hutchings. It finally got the name of William Pitt Ballinger, a Galveston lawyer who held railroad stock.
Spot A
Runnels City lost the county seat to us in 1888. In 1900 about eleven hundred people lived here. By 1910 it was three and a half thousand. In 1909, Andrew Carnegie sent twelve thousand five hundred dollars to build the library. It's one of only three of Texas's original Carnegie libraries still working as a full service library. [OWNER: one memory of that library, or a story about it] Our agency opened its doors in 1903, about seventeen years after that lot sale. [OWNER: a family you insure whose people have been in Ballinger since the early days]
3
When you have a claim, who picks up the phone?
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
The agency's own site promises local claims handling and a familiar face. Laid out as a question every buyer should ask, it sets the standard without naming a company.
Spot C
Before you buy any insurance policy, ask one question. When I have a claim, who do I call, and do they know my name? Some companies will give you a number and a ticket. Here, you get Debbie, Terilyn, Jessica or me.
Spot A
Our team averages more than twenty years of Texas insurance experience. We handle claims locally. Somebody you already know walks you through it. If it happens after hours, there's a phone line for claims help. That matters on the night a tree comes through the roof, or a truck backs into your trailer. [OWNER: a claim you remember walking a customer through, with the names left out]
Spot B
We're independent, too, which means we work with more than one company. If one carrier raises your rate, we can look at the others. An agent who sells for only one company can only offer you that company. So the question to ask your agent is simple. How many companies can you shop for me, and who answers when I call about a claim? We serve Ballinger, Abilene, San Angelo, Brady and Brownwood, all from this one office. If the answer is good, stay where you are. If it isn't, we're at 112 South 7th Street.
4
Does your auto insurance follow the car or the driver?
The useful one
▾
A question people argue about at every family barbecue, answered by the agency's own agent in her own blog post. It earns the follow and shows the staff know their stuff.
Spot A
Quick question. When your buddy borrows your truck and wrecks it, whose insurance pays? Most people guess it follows the driver. In most cases it follows the car.
Spot B
Terilyn wrote about this on our blog, and she's right. If you gave them permission, your policy on that vehicle usually pays first. Their own insurance may come in behind it if the damage runs past your limits. That's why your liability limits matter most when other people drive your vehicle. If you borrow a friend's car and wreck it, the same rule runs the other way. Their policy generally goes first, and yours can back it up. There are exceptions, and they matter. A driver you've excluded from your policy isn't covered. Somebody who lives in your house and drives your car every week should be listed on the policy.
Spot C
Business use is the other one. A regular personal policy generally doesn't cover deliveries or driving customers around. Ride share and delivery apps usually need an endorsement added. So before you hand over the keys, check three things. Do they have a license and a clean record. Are your limits high enough. Is everyone who drives it listed. If you don't know, call us at 325 365 2516 and we'll look it up.
