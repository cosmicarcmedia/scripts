1
I spent my career explaining towns to the people moving into them
The origin and the face
▾
He did the job of introducing a town to newcomers for years, at four different chambers. Saying so on camera turns a crowded field of agents into one man who knows the whole place.
Spot A
For most of my career, I explained towns to the people moving into them. I started at the Midland Chamber of Commerce in 1989. Then I ran the chamber in Nacogdoches for fourteen years. Then Longmont, Colorado. In 2018, my wife Linda and I moved to San Angelo, and I spent my first months meeting business owners and learning the town.
Spot B
Before any of that, I studied journalism at Oklahoma State, and then geology at UT Permian Basin. I've worked as a news photographer, a writer, and an earth scientist. So I'm the guy who asks a lot of questions, and wants to know what's under the ground and why a town sits where it does. That curiosity never wore off. Spot C
Now I'm a REALTOR with NextHome Centurion Realty, helping people buy and sell at every price point. Linda and I love history, the performing arts and the visual arts, and we like to hike and bike all over West Central Texas. When you're thinking about a move here, you're choosing a whole place to live, with its history, its weather, and its people. I'm glad to tell you about all of it, and I've had practice.
2
San Angelo is named for a man's wife, and nobody agrees which one
The local anchor
▾
A piece of town history almost nobody in a town of this size has heard, told by a former chamber executive. It is built to be sent along with a 'did you know' and it plants him as the local who knows the story.
Spot C
Here's a San Angelo story most people here have never heard. In the late 1860s, a man named Bart DeWitt bought three hundred and twenty acres for a dollar an acre. It sat across the river from Fort Concho, which opened in 1867. He set up a trading post for the soldiers. A local historian found that he named the town after his wife, Carolina Angela, who died in 1866.
Spot A
The name was San Angela at first. When the post office application went in during 1883, somebody pointed out that San Angela was not grammatical, so it became San Angelo. By then Ben Ficklin, the old county seat, was gone. A flood in 1882 had washed away the courthouse, and the county seat moved here.
Spot B
The railroad came in 1888, and the town took off. Sheep and wool made it the biggest sheep market in the country. Then in 1940 the War Department picked this ground for a flying school. That became Goodfellow Air Force Base, named for John Goodfellow Jr., a San Angelo man killed in France in World War I. So if you get orders to Goodfellow, you're landing in a town that has been welcoming soldiers since before it had a name.
3
Your agent knows the house. Ask if they know the town
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
The sharpest of the four, and the one that gets forwarded. It names the gap between an agent who works from a listing and one who spent a career sitting in rooms where a town's plans got made, without naming any brokerage.
Spot B
When you're hiring an agent, you'll hear a lot about the house. Square footage, the updates, the comps. All of that matters, and I do it too. But the house is one piece of the decision. A house sits in a neighborhood, and a neighborhood sits in a city, and a city is always planning something.
Spot C
So ask your agent three things. What's going on in this part of town over the next few years. Who has to approve it. And what would change about the price if it happens. Plenty of agents will smile and go back to the kitchen. A good answer to those three says they have done their homework. I spent years in rooms where those plans got talked about before anyone printed them.
Spot A
Every franchise will tell you about their national marketing. I'll tell you about San Angelo. I spent years working with the businesses, the schools and the base, and I got to know the people who run them, and I'm still meeting the same people. I'm Bruce Partain with NextHome Centurion Realty. If you want somebody who knows this town as well as the house, call me before you pick a street. I'll show you the house and the town around it.
4
Before you buy in San Angelo, check where the three rivers go
The useful one
▾
Free, useful and tied to the town's real history. It is the sort of thing a Goodfellow family with a short house hunt will not think to ask, and it earns him the follow.
Spot C
If you're house hunting in San Angelo, check one thing the listing won't show you. This town sits where the North, South and Middle Concho rivers meet. That's why Fort Concho was built here, and it's why the county seat was washed away in 1882, after a flood took out the courthouse in Ben Ficklin. It's a good town and I love it, and it makes sense to look at the flood map before you fall for a yard.
Spot A
Second, drive the neighborhood three times. Once on a weekday morning, once after school lets out, and once on a Saturday night. The street you see on a Sunday afternoon isn't always the street you live on. Third, find out how far you are from the places you'll use every day, including the base if you're coming in on orders.
Spot B
Fourth, ask what a house sold for on that street over the last few years. Look at what they sold for once the paperwork was done, because that is the number that counts. I can pull that for you in a few minutes. If you're moving with the military, I hold the Military Relocation Professional designation, so I know your timeline is tight. I'm Bruce Partain with NextHome Centurion Realty. Walk it with me before you make an offer, and bring your questions.
