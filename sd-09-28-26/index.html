1
The route my dad bought from his own uncle
Origin
▾
Almost nobody buying propane in San Angelo today knows the business started as a delivery route in Miles in 1935, run by a nephew for his own uncle, then bought outright five years later. That specific, sourced detail is worth more than any generic family owned since line.
Spot A
In 1935, my dad, Bill Schneider, came out to West Texas to work for his uncle, Ed Jansen, delivering Gulf Oil products in a little town called Miles. He wasn't running the business. He was just driving the route for family. There was no guarantee any of it would ever belong to him. Uncles don't owe that to a nephew. By 1940, he and my mother Jake had bought that route from him outright.
Spot B
In 1952 my dad added propane to what we sold, and we became Schneider LP Gas Service. By 1955 we'd grown past Miles into all of San Angelo. I grew up watching every bit of that happen. In 1968, after college, my husband Jerome and I came home and joined the business ourselves, instead of building a life somewhere else.
Spot C
My dad stayed active in this company until he passed away in 2000. We incorporated it as Schneider Distributing in 1980, but the paperwork was never really the point. Three generations of this family have run trucks out of this same business now. It isn't only family either. John Walter came on the same year we incorporated, back in 1980, and he's still running operations for us forty five years later. Almost ninety years in. Still the same family. Still West Texas.
2
This started in Miles, not San Angelo
Local anchor
▾
This company reads as a San Angelo business today, but its first twenty years belonged entirely to Miles. Naming that, and tying propane to how rural West Texas homes and barns actually get heated, roots the business in a place most competitors cannot claim.
Spot B
People assume we're a San Angelo company, because that's where the retail station sits today, on South Abe Street. That's only half true, and it's the newer half. This whole thing started in Miles, back in 1935, before propane was even part of the business. Ninety years later, our truck still rolls out toward Miles on plenty of days, same as it did back then.
Spot A
San Angelo didn't come into it until 1955, twenty years after my dad's first route. Out here, propane isn't some backup option people choose. A lot of the houses and barns in this part of Texas don't have a gas line running to them at all. Propane is how they get heated, full stop. When a hard freeze comes through West Texas, and out here it always eventually does, those are the houses that call us first, not last.
Spot C
So when we say we've been doing this since 1935, that's not a slogan somebody printed on a sign. That's twenty years in Miles before San Angelo ever entered the picture, and ninety years now of knowing exactly what this part of Texas actually runs on, town and ranch both. We never forgot which town came first.
3
You can walk into our shop. Try that with an 800 number.
Competitor contrast
▾
A ninety year old family business with an actual walk-in retail counter is a genuinely different experience than an 800 number and a dispatch truck. This draws that contrast in general terms, since no specific national competitor was confirmed operating in this footprint.
Spot C
A lot of propane companies today are just a phone number and a dispatch truck. You call, you wait, and somebody you've never met shows up eventually.
Spot A
We still run an actual retail station, on South Abe Street, where you can walk in the door. Get your bottle filled right there, anywhere from five pounds up to a hundred. Get it inspected, recertified, or repaired while you wait. Buy the grill or the heater that runs on it, from the same counter. Need a hose nobody stocks. We build custom ones right there in house. We fill forklift cylinders for businesses all over town too, not just backyard grills.
Spot B
We also carry fuels a residential-only supplier never bothers to stock, red diesel and K1 kerosene, because a lot of our customers are running tractors and barns, not just a house. Ninety years in the same family means we've never once had to guess what this town and the ranches around it actually need. That's not something a call center in another state can offer, no matter how nice the person on the phone is. When you call here, you're usually talking to somebody who already knows your name, not reading it off a screen for the first time.
4
Your propane tank has an expiration date. Here's where to find it.
Useful customer education
▾
Most propane customers have never been told their tank has an expiration stamped on the collar and needs to be recertified, or that a supplier can legally refuse to fill an expired one. Costs nothing to explain and it is exactly the kind of thing this business already does on site.
Spot A
If you own a propane tank, it has an expiration date stamped right on the collar, and most people have never once looked for it. It's usually just two numbers, the month and the year of the last inspection, sometimes with a letter after it for how that inspection was done.
Spot B
Propane tanks have to be recertified periodically. Ten years for the first stamp, then every five years after that. If that date has passed, a supplier can legally refuse to fill it, and should. It isn't somebody being difficult. It's the same rule everywhere, not just here. A lot of people hear expired and think that means buying a whole new tank. Most of the time it just means a fresh inspection, five or ten more years stamped onto the same one. We do that inspection and recertification right at our station on South Abe Street, usually while you wait.
Spot C
So before your next fill up, go look at the collar of your tank for that date stamp. If you can't find it, or can't read it anymore, bring it in and let somebody check it for you. That's a five minute stop that keeps a small problem from turning into a dangerous one, and it's the same five minutes whether you bought that tank from us or somewhere else.
