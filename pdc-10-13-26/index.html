3
How does he know your pond will hold water?
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
These are the questions a landowner should ask any contractor, taken from the business's own pond, road and septic pages. It draws a fair line without naming anyone, and it is the one a rancher forwards to his neighbor.
Spot B
Here's a question to ask any contractor who offers to dig you a pond. How do you know it'll hold water before you build it? If the answer is we'll see, that's an expensive way to find out. We dig test holes first. We check the clay, and we check whether your place gets enough rainwater runoff to fill it. That's how we know the finished pond will hold water.
Spot C
Ask the same thing about a cleanout. Before we touch a pond that's silted in, we test how deep the silt is and mark where it comes out. Then ask about your road. Is the base watered and compacted, and does it drain? Ours is, and it does. Ask the same about a building pad. Is it level, watered, and compacted? Ask where the silt is going to go. Ask whether they looked at the dirt already on your property before they quoted you a load of material.
Spot A
And on a septic system, ask to see the license. We're a licensed on-site sewage facility installer with the TCEQ. We install a range of system types, so ask what fits your lot. None of this is a secret. If a contractor can't answer these, keep looking. If you want it checked and done right, call me.
4
How to tell if your ground will hold a pond
The useful one
▾
Anybody with land and a low spot has wondered about a pond. This gives away the clay check and the runoff math for free, and it earns the call from the person who is about to spend real money.
Spot A
If you're thinking about a pond, squeeze a handful of dirt first. Take it moist, and press it into a ball. If the ball holds its shape, that's a good sign. If it falls apart, you're probably looking at sand or gravel, and sand won't hold water. The rule of thumb is soil with at least twenty percent clay.
Spot B
Soil can change a lot on one piece of land. So check in more than one spot, and above all where the dam is going to sit. The dam needs a high clay content, and it should go up in layers, six to eight inches at a time, with each layer compacted. The dirt has to be the right moisture to pack, too. Too dry and it won't hold together. Too wet and it sticks to the equipment.
Spot C
Then look at the water coming in. A pasture with heavy clay soil may give you an acre of water for every five acres of land that drains into it. Too little runoff and the pond never fills. Too much and it can silt in fast. That's why we dig test holes before we build one, to check the clay and the runoff. If you've got a spot in mind, call me, and we'll talk it through.
