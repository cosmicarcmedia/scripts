1
The morning we found Gracie the giraffe, and how Concho Aviation got here
The origin and the face
▾
Hardly anyone knows that the helicopter that found Gracie the giraffe out of Real County is from Sterling City. Leading with that story and then walking back to 1985 and one Robinson R22 puts a face and a history on a business that has neither online. It also hands ranchers a reason to remember the name.
Spot A
This summer, a ranch in Real County needed help finding a giraffe. Her name was Gracie. She'd climbed out over a steep rock slab with no fence on it, and she'd been missing almost two weeks. The ranch had already searched about seventy five hundred acres from the air, with no luck. Vick Jones from the ranch and I flew a search early one morning, south of Cedar Hollow Ranch, and about four miles out, there she was. Healthy, and calm. The sheriff posted the news on Facebook, and it spread from there. I'm Jeff Hill, chief pilot at Concho Aviation.
Spot B
We're out of Sterling City, and we've been here since 1985. Mackey McEntire, a fourth generation Sterling City rancher, started this company with one Robinson R22. That was the whole fleet. More than forty years later, we're still a Sterling City company. [OWNER: one line about Mackey that you or Cathy want in this video, or cut this part] In 2016 I came on as a partner. I'm the chief pilot and I manage operations.
Spot C
Today we fly R22s, R44s and R66s, with several pilots and two mechanics on the team. We gather cattle, goats and wild horses, we fly predator control on hogs and coyotes, and we run wildlife surveys. [OWNER: how long you've been flying, and what first brought you to ranch work] The giraffe made the news. A lot of our calls are a rancher with a herd to move.

From Sterling City to Rankin to Andrews, with a horseman on the ground
The local anchor

Home base is Sterling City, in Sterling County. That's where the office is, and where the phone rings. But the work goes a long way past the county line. 3
Four questions to ask before you hire a helicopter for your ranch
The thing the big competitor will not say
▾
Ranchers hire a helicopter on trust, and trust is hard to read from a phone call. These four questions give the viewer a checklist for any outfit and let Concho answer each from its own pages: mechanics, aircraft, safety, response. No company is named.
Spot C
Before you hire anybody to work your ranch from a helicopter, ask four things. One. Do you have your own mechanics? We do. Two of them, working on our aircraft. That matters, because a helicopter waiting on a repair isn't moving your herd. Two. How many pilots, and how many helicopters? We have several pilots, and a fleet of R22s, R44s and R66s.
Spot A
Three. Which helicopter are you sending, and why? We match the helicopter to the ground. The smaller R22 handles the standard work, and the R44 comes in when the terrain calls for it. That matters to you, because the bigger one costs more to fly. If a company can't tell you which aircraft shows up, that tells you something. [OWNER: what you charge per flight hour for each helicopter]
Spot B
Four. What happens before anybody picks up a gun? On our predator control flights, hunters and pilots go through in depth gun safety training first. Safety is the top priority for both, and it says so right on our website. Some mornings the fog sits on the ground, and we wait for it to lift. That's four questions. Ask them anywhere, and listen to how fast the answers come. We've been doing this out of Sterling City since 1985, and we'll answer all four.
4
What a helicopter deer survey tells you, and what it can't
The useful one
▾
Landowners who lease for hunting want a deer number, and the Texas Parks and Wildlife guidance on helicopter surveys answers the question plainly, including where the count falls short. Concho flies wildlife surveys, so this teaches the buyer how to use one and quietly says who can do it.
Spot A
If you lease your ranch for hunting, you've wondered how many deer are out there. A helicopter survey is one way to find out. Here's how it works, going by Texas Parks and Wildlife's own guidance. The helicopter flies thirty to fifty feet off the ground, in straight lines across each pasture. It starts fifteen to thirty minutes after sunrise. A pilot and an observer both count, and they log the bucks, the does and the fawns. A good survey also shows you where the deer are living, pasture by pasture.
Spot B
Here's the catch. A survey count is a sample. It misses deer. Studies from Texas A&M Kingsville found that helicopter surveys in South Texas can undercount deer by sixty to seventy percent. So you treat the number as a trend, and you use it to plan your harvest, year after year.
Spot C
Two practical things. First, Texas requires a landowner authorization form, filed with Parks and Wildlife before anybody flies your ranch. [OWNER: how you help a landowner with that form] Second, timing. On country with a lot of mesquite, you wait until the leaves drop, late fall into winter, so the deer can be seen. We fly thousands of acres a year in wildlife work. If you want your ranch counted, call three two five, three seven eight, two zero five one.
